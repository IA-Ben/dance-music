Forty years of dance music · 1986–2026

Dance
Music

Ben Dawson

In 1986 a few hundred people in Chicago, Detroit and a theatre in Camden were dancing to records made on drum machines nobody else wanted. Forty years later the music they were hearing is the sound of every city on earth. This book is a celebration of everything that happened in between — the rooms, the records, the machines and the people — built so that it can be read in any order and played out loud.

Forty years, one page a year

The spine of the celebration is the Forty years strand: a page for every year from 1986, year one, to 2026, forty years on — what was released, which rooms opened, who arrived, the sound of that year from the engine, and a photograph. Start there if you want the party; start with a strand if you want the argument.

Why it is a network and not a line

Histories of this music are usually written as one line: Kraftwerk, Detroit, Chicago, Ibiza, London, Berlin. But the music did not happen in a line. A record pressed on the South Side of Chicago was played in a Southwark warehouse in 1988 by a milkman from Mile End, heard by a kid who would open a club in a vault under the Berlin Wall, and is still being played thirty-five years later by a seaside crew who named themselves after a Detroit militia. The connections are the history. So the book is built as the thing it describes: a graph. Every person, club, record, label, machine, radio station, event and landmark set is a page of its own, and every page is linked to everything it touches — both ways. Follow a DJ to the rooms they played; a room to the records made for it; a record to the machine it was made on; the machine back out to everyone who used it. Each page shows its neighbourhood as a map, the chapters that mention it, and the trail you took to reach it.

Strands and crossings

A graph is not readable on its own, so five strands run through it as spines of chapters you can turn like a book: Techno, House, Rave, Jungle & drum and bass and Trance. They are told separately on purpose — techno and house are different musics with a shared grid, and rave is a culture rather than a genre — but they keep meeting. Where they share a place or a moment, a crossing page tells it from each side and lets you change strand: Chicago, acid, Ibiza, the 1992 split, Castlemorton, tech-house. Chapters end in forks that say where each path goes next.

The sound

Because this is music, the book makes sound. Anatomy takes a techno track apart layer by layer — kick, drums, percussion, rumble, bass, synth, sampling, mix — with every sound synthesised live in the browser rather than played from a recording. Mixer puts it back together: two synced decks, a genre preset for every dialect from Chicago 1986 to hard techno 2023, and an auto-set that mixes itself through a strand with bass swaps and tempo ramps. Every history chapter and most genre pages carry a "hear the era" button that loads the matching preset.

Techno · 00

Forty years in one picture

Techno was made in Detroit by three school friends with German records and Japanese machines, sold to Britain as the sound of the future, and given a home in East Berlin the year the Wall came down. Everything since has been a conversation between those three cities and the ones that joined later — Birmingham, Frankfurt, Ghent, Tokyo, Tbilisi. This is a history of techno only: house, its closest relative, has its own strand, and the genres that branched off get their own chapter at the end of this part.

Techno · 00a · 1920–1980

Prehistory: the electronic century

Before there was a 909 there were sixty years of machines that made sound without strings or reeds, and a handful of people who worked out what to do with them. Techno's roots chapter picks the story up in 1974; this page is the ground under it.

Instruments and techniques, 1920–1980. Everything techno uses — oscillators, sequencing, tape loops, the vocoder, the drum machine — exists on this line before 1960.

1920s–50s: instruments without players

Léon Theremin's instrument (1920) was played without being touched; Maurice Martenot's Ondes (1928) put an oscillator under a keyboard; Trautwein and Sala's Trautonium (1930) used neon-tube oscillators and a wire over a rail. In 1931 Theremin built the Rhythmicon for Henry Cowell — multiple simultaneous rhythms from a machine, which makes it the first drum machine. Hammond's Novachord (1938) was a polyphonic synthesiser with 163 vacuum tubes; Homer Dudley's vocoder (1940s) was a telephone-company speech compressor that would end up on Trans-Europe Express; and magnetic tape, from the late 1940s, gave Pierre Schaeffer's musique concrète its medium: recorded sound cut into loops, slowed, reversed, reassembled — sampling, forty years early. RCA's Mark II (1957) sequenced a synthesiser from punch cards.

1960s: the workshop and the Moog

The BBC Radiophonic Workshop (1958) made electronic sound a public memory — Delia Derbyshire's realisation of the Doctor Who theme (1963) from tape and test oscillators — while Stockhausen in Cologne and Schaeffer in Paris treated it as composition. Robert Moog's modular (1964) and Wendy Carlos's Switched-On Bach (1968) made synthesis a thing people bought records of.

1970s: into the mainstream

Synthesisers reached rock (Pink Floyd, Yes, ELP) and, more consequentially for this book, reached repetition: Pete Townshend's looping Lowrey organ on "Baba O'Riley" (1971), Tangerine Dream's sequencer patterns, Eno's ambient tape systems, and Kraftwerk's Autobahn (1974), where a German band decided the machine was the band. Giorgio Moroder's "I Feel Love" (1977) then put a Moog sequencer under a disco vocal and produced the first dance record whose groove was entirely mechanical. From there the Roots chapter takes over: Roland's three boxes, the Bronx, and a radio show in Detroit.

Techno · 01 · 1974–1985

Before techno: machines, disco, Chicago

Nothing about techno was invented from nothing. The machines came from Japan, the idea of machine music from Düsseldorf, the dancefloor from New York and Chicago, and the word from a futurologist's paperback.

Düsseldorf: the machine as a band

Kraftwerk's Autobahn (1974), Trans-Europe Express (1977) and Computer World (1981) proved that sequencers and drum machines could carry pop music on their own, with no rock band underneath. More importantly for Detroit, they reached American radio. In 1982 Afrika Bambaataa built "Planet Rock" on Trans-Europe Express and Numbers, and electro — the direct ancestor of techno — was born in the Bronx out of German parts.

The neighbour: Chicago house

Techno's closest relative was being made 280 miles west at the same time. Chicago house — disco's continuation in the Warehouse and the Music Box, drum-machine tracks by the club's own crowd from 1984, and from 1987 the squealing 303 of acid — gave Detroit's DJs records to play, a label network to sell into, and the four-on-the-floor grid both musics share. It is a different music with a different history, and it has its own strand in this book. Where it touches techno directly — acid, the UK's conflation of the two, tech-house — the chapters say so.

Giorgio Moroder, 1977

Donna Summer's "I Feel Love", built almost entirely on a Moog modular sequencer, is the other founding document: a whole dance record played by a machine, throbbing, with the bass on the offbeat. Brian Eno famously told David Bowie it was the sound of the next fifteen years. He undershot.

Detroit radio

Charles Johnson — The Electrifying Mojo — broadcast on WGPR and then WJLB through the late 1970s and 80s, mixing Kraftwerk, Parliament-Funkadelic, Prince, the B-52s and Gary Numan into a single late-night universe. Three teenagers from Belleville, a suburb 30 miles west of the city, were listening.

The word

Juan Atkins took "techno" from Alvin Toffler's The Third Wave (1980), which described "techno-rebels" who would use technology on their own terms rather than be used by it. Atkins and Rick Davis called themselves Cybotron; their 1983 single "Clear" is the first Detroit record that sounds like what followed.

The Roland trio

Roland built the TR-808 (1980), TB-303 (1981) and TR-909 (1983) as rehearsal accompaniment for working musicians. All three were commercial failures — musicians found them unrealistic — and by 1985 they were in pawn shops for a few hundred dollars, which is exactly how teenagers in Chicago and Detroit afforded them. The 808 is the sound of electro and hip-hop; the 909 is the sound of house and techno; the 303 is the sound of acid. Section 01 of the Anatomy part shows how each is built.

Techno · 02 · 1985–1992

Detroit: hi-tech soul

Three friends from Belleville High School, a radio DJ called The Wizard, a club with no alcohol licence, and a compilation that gave the music its name. Detroit made techno in about five years, then spent the next thirty reminding everyone.

The Belleville Three

Juan Atkins, Derrick May and Kevin Saunderson met at school in Belleville, Michigan, and DJ'd together as Deep Space Soundworks. Atkins went first: after Cybotron he founded Metroplex and released Model 500's "No UFO's" (1985), stripped-down electro-funk that is usually called the first techno record. May's Transmat label followed with "Nude Photo" and "Strings of Life" (both 1987, as Rhythim Is Rhythim), the latter built from Michael James's piano riff and a 909 with no bassline at all. Saunderson's KMS put Detroit in the pop charts: Inner City's "Big Fun" and "Good Life" (1988, with Paris Grey) were UK Top 10 hits.

Juan Atkins died on 31 October 2026, aged 64, found unresponsive in his Mexico City hotel room before a set at YuYu Cine Club. He had been touring to the end — Movement, Dekmantel, Houghton. Kevin Saunderson, the next morning: "Juan was the originator of techno, a true visionary who opened the door for so many of us." Every page in this strand descends from the two records he made in a bedroom in Belleville.

Naming it, 1988

Neil Rushton, a Birmingham soul DJ working for Virgin's 10 Records, came to Detroit to license a compilation of what he assumed was the city's house music. The working title was The House Sound of Detroit. Atkins contributed a track called "Techno Music", the title changed to Techno! The New Dance Sound of Detroit, and a genre had a name — though Derrick May's description stuck harder: George Clinton and Kraftwerk stuck in a lift.

The Music Institute, 1988–89

At 1315 Broadway, downtown, George Baker, Alton Miller and Chez Damier opened a club modelled on the Paradise Garage and the Music Box: a juice bar, no alcohol, members only, midnight until nine in the morning. Derrick May's Friday residency was where Detroit heard its own records for the first time alongside Chicago and the new British acid imports. It lasted eighteen months — May 1988 to 24 November 1989 — and May closed the final night with "Strings of Life".

Underground Resistance

In 1989–90 "Mad" Mike Banks and Jeff Mills, soon joined by Robert Hood, founded Underground Resistance: masked, anonymous, militant, refusing interviews and major labels, releasing harder and faster records than the first wave — "The Final Frontier", "Riot", later Galaxy 2 Galaxy's "Jupiter Jazz" — and framing techno explicitly as Black working-class resistance in a deindustrialising city. Mills, who had been The Wizard on WJLB radio since the mid-80s, left in 1992 to found Axis and become the genre's most influential DJ; Hood's "Minimal Nation" (1994) invented minimal techno. Banks is still there, at Submerge on East Grand Boulevard.

The second wave

Carl Craig (Planet E, 1991; "Bug in the Bassbin"), Richie Hawtin and John Acquaviva across the river in Windsor, Ontario (Plus 8, 1990; Plastikman's Sheet One and "Spastik", 1993), Kenny Larkin, Stacey Pullen, Claude Young, Octave One, and Drexciya — James Stinson and Gerald Donald's electro mythology of an undersea nation descended from the Middle Passage. Afrofuturism, the automation of the Ford plants, the 1967 uprising and the emptied city are all in the records whether or not anyone says so.

The city underneath

The music came out of a specific Detroit: the auto industry collapsing under foreign competition and successive recessions; a Black working class bearing the job losses; the memory of the 1967 uprising still fresh; Coleman Young, the city's first Black mayor, in office through the whole decade; and the national War on Drugs filling prisons with the same young men. Atkins cited Parliament-Funkadelic and Sun Ra as much as Kraftwerk, and Cybotron's "Techno City" and "Cosmic Cars" are explicitly Afrofuturist — a future claimed by people the present had written off. Techno was wordless on purpose: a universal language for a city that had been left behind.

Techno · 03 · 1988–1999

Britain: techno's first export market

Britain was the first country outside Detroit to buy techno, name it, and make its own. The house explosion that built the audience in 1987–88 — Shoom, the Haçienda, the Second Summer of Love — is house history and lives in that strand. What Britain then did with Detroit's records is this chapter: bleep, the free-party sound systems, the Orbit, Lost, Birmingham, and the hardest DJs of the 1990s.

1988: the word arrives from Birmingham

Neil Rushton, a Northern Soul DJ from Birmingham working for Virgin's 10 Records, went to Detroit expecting to license house. The compilation he came back with, Techno! The New Dance Sound of Detroit, named the genre; his own Network Records then put Detroit on British shelves for a decade — Model 500, Reese, Inner City, the Bio Rhythm compilations. Inner City's chart hits paid for it. The British press treated techno as a flavour of acid house for two years; the records said otherwise.

Sheffield bleep, 1989–91

Warp Records opened in Sheffield in late 1989 from the FON shop, and its first records — Sweet Exorcist's "Testone", LFO's "LFO" (UK No. 12 in 1990), Nightmares on Wax's "Aftermath", Forgemasters — fused Detroit minimalism with the sub-bass of Yorkshire reggae sound systems. Bleep was the first distinctly British techno and the first techno in which the bass sat below the kick. Warp then left for Aphex Twin, Autechre and "intelligent" techno — a branch of its own.

1990–92: the rave split

In the rave halls techno meant Belgium and the Netherlands — "Energy Flash", "Mentasm", "Dominator", "Anasthasia" — played at 140 next to breakbeats. By 1992 the breakbeat side had become hardcore and was about to become jungle; the straight-4/4 side re-separated into a purist strand with its own rooms: Lost (Steve Bicknell and Sheree Rashit, 1991 —, Jeff Mills's London home), the Orbit at After Dark in Morley near Leeds (1991–2003, where every Detroit, Berlin and Birmingham name played a Yorkshire mill town), Final Frontier at Club UK (1993), Eurobeat 2000, and Colin Dale and Colin Faver's shows on Kiss FM.

The sound systems and acid techno

Spiral Tribe — founded in 1990 by Mark Harrison, Debbie Griffith, Simone and others, with the mantra "make some noise" and later their own SP23 label — took techno out of licensed rooms and into fields, squats and disused airfields, playing hard, fast, acid-driven sets through their own rig and aligning the free party with the travellers, squatters and road protesters who had been fighting the state since the Battle of the Beanfield (1985); the week-long free festival at Castlemorton Common in May 1992 provoked the Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994, which criminalised gatherings of music "wholly or predominantly characterised by the emission of a succession of repetitive beats". The Tribe, facing prosecution, went to France and started the teknival, spreading the free-party ethos across the continent; London kept the sound in Stay Up Forever (1993 — Chris and Julian Liberator, D.A.V.E. the Drummer): a 303 and a 909 at 140, the acid techno that is still the city's free-party dialect.

Birmingham and the hard line

House of God (1993) at the Que Club and Karl O'Connor's Downwards label (1993) gave Birmingham a techno that owed as much to Throbbing Gristle and Cabaret Voltaire as to Detroit. Regis, Surgeon, Female and later Sandwell District made the most uncompromising British techno of the decade; Atomic Jam (1995–) at the Que Club was its big room. The Birmingham sound is the direct ancestor of the industrial revival of the 2010s.

Scotland and the rest

Slam's Sub Club in Glasgow (1987) and their Soma label (1991), Pure in Edinburgh (1990–2001, Twitch and Brainstorm, where Jeff Mills and Dave Clarke were regulars), Rubadub's shop (1992); Dave Clarke's "Red" trilogy (1994–95) from Brighton; Luke Slater's Peacefrog years and Planetary Assault Systems; Cristian Vogel and Neil Landstrumm's Tresor and Mosquito records; Mark Broom and Ben Sims's hardgroove (1997–); Bugged Out in Manchester (1994); Tribal Gathering's Detroit stage (1995–97). By the time fabric opened in October 1999, Britain had a techno infrastructure as deep as Germany's and a tenth as visible.

Techno · 04 · 1986–2026

London: from Clink Street to The End, and out to the coast

London is in this book three times already — Shoom and the Ibiza Four in the house strand, the M25 and the pirates in rave — but it also has a techno story of its own, and it starts before acid house had a name. It runs from the Camden Palace to a warehouse beside the old Clink prison, through the pirate stations and the purist rooms of the early 90s, into a Victorian horse stable on West Central Street that became The End, and then — this is the part nobody planned — fifty miles down the A21 to a seaside town that now trades DJs with Detroit.

1986–88: the Palace and the prison

Before there were raves there was Colin Faver and Eddie Richards's residency at the Camden Palace, where Faver played Detroit and Chicago imports to a mixed, mostly straight-from-work crowd and a 21-year-old East End rapper called Richard West — Mr. C — was the resident MC from 1986, then MC'd for Faver on the still-illegal Kiss FM. In August 1987 West and Richards recorded a deep house track, "Page 67", as Myster-E; by September he had quit his milk round and taken to the decks. Through 1988 he was resident every Friday and Saturday at RIP — Paul Stone and Lu Vukovic's parties at Clink Street, a warehouse on the Southwark river-front beside the old prison. RIP was the dark twin of Shoom: no smiley faces, harder Chicago, the first Detroit records, Richards and Kid Batchelor alongside C, and the police outside. If Shoom is where London fell in love with house, Clink Street is where it learned that some of these records were something else.

C's own parties ran in a straight line from there — Fantasy (1988), Base at the Dungeons on Lea Bridge Road (1989), Release (1991), Harmony (1992), Drop (1993), Cyclone (1994), Vapourspace (1994–95) — while The Shamen, with him as frontman, took "Move Any Mountain" and "Ebeneezer Goode" (UK No. 1, 1992) into the charts. The pop money built the club.

1990–95: the pirates and the purist rooms

Kiss went legal in 1990 and gave techno two weekly hours it had nowhere else: Colin Faver's show and Colin Dale's Abstrakt Dance, with Mr. C's Future Music following on Kiss 100 from 1993 to 2002. In the rooms, the straight-4/4 side of the rave split found its homes: Lost (1991 —, Steve Bicknell and Sheree Rashit's warehouse night, where Jeff Mills played London), Final Frontier at Club UK (1993), Eurobeat 2000, and Trade at Turnmills on Sunday mornings — the gay after-hours that hardened house into its own thing and is told in the house strand. Eddie Richards's Dy-na-mix agency (1990) put the same DJs on every flyer. The London dialect that came out of it — house's swing with techno's sound design, no vocal — got its name from Terry Francis and Nathan Coles's Wiggle parties (1994 —) and C's Plink Plonk label (1993): tech-house, the crossing both strands claim.

December 1995 – January 2009: The End

Layo Paskin's father Douglas, an architect, found a nineteenth-century stable for the Post Office's mail horses at 18 West Central Street, behind New Oxford Street, and designed a club into it. Mr. C and Layo opened The End in December 1995 with the booth on the floor, facing the dancers, and a sound system specified by DJs rather than decorators. C's Subterrain (1995–2002) and Flavour were the house nights; Layo & Bushwacka! were the residents and End Recordings (1996 —) the label — "Love Story" (2002) its hit; AKA, the bar next door, opened in 1998; Superfreq, C's party and label, started in 2002 and ran monthly until the last night. Detroit was a regular guest — Derrick May, Kevin Saunderson, Carl Craig — and the Monday-night Trash made Erol Alkan. The End and AKA closed on 24 January 2009 with a 24-hour party. Most of what London clubs have done since — fabric's booth, the no-décor concrete rooms, the system-first design — was first done there.

1999–2026: fabric, the sheds and the coast

fabric opened under Smithfield in October 1999 with Craig Richards and Terry Francis as residents — Wiggle's tech-house in a three-room club with a vibrating floor; Corsica Studios (2002) under the railway at Elephant and Castle became the small room that mattered; then the warehouse decade: Printworks in the Canada Water press halls (2017–23), Fold in Canning Town (2018 —, a 24-hour licence and no phones), Drumsheds in the old Tottenham IKEA. Stay Up Forever's acid techno never went away. And in 2023 the story left the city.

Hastings ⇄ Detroit, 2023–

Hastings, the Sussex fishing town that produced John Digweed, got a techno scene the old way — a promoter and a room. Catchmydrift? brought Detroit Techno Militia, the street-level Detroit DJ-and-label collective (T.Linder, DJ Seoul, Darkcube, Neil V, J.Garcia; the DTM 5x5 of five DJs on five turntables; the Made in Detroit stage at Movement 2011), to Hastings on Easter Sunday 2023; back again on 27 December 2024 at The Pig, on a run that went Hastings → Edinburgh → DJ Bone's Further in Amsterdam for New Year's Eve; and again on Easter Sunday 2026 at the Deep End in the Brewing Brothers Courtyard. The local residents — DJK, Japhy, Rob Miller — took the name and became Hastings Techno Militia, a vinyl crew playing Detroit records in a seaside town and sending DJK to play Detroit and come back with the new wax. It is the same exchange that opened this chapter: Rushton to Detroit in 1988, Mills to Lost in 1991, May to West Central Street, the 313 to the Sussex coast. Techno has always been a conversation between a city and the places that loved its records.

Techno · 05 · 1988–1999

Berlin: the vault and the empty city

The Wall fell on 9 November 1989 and left half a city empty: bank vaults, power stations, department stores, bunkers, with no owners and no police. Within sixteen months Detroit's records had a building, and techno had a capital.

Before the Wall fell

UFO (1988–90), run by Dimitri Hegemann's Interfisch label with Carola Stoiber and Matthias Roeingh — Dr. Motte — was West Berlin's first acid house club, in a Kreuzberg basement, with Tanith — later resident at Tresor and E-Werk, the city's first techno DJ — Jonzon, Kid Paul and Rok on the decks. On 1 July 1989 Motte and Danielle de Picciotto registered a political demonstration on the Kurfürstendamm, put a sound system on a truck and called it the Love Parade. A hundred and fifty people came. Two thousand came in 1990; by the end of the decade it was a million and a half in the Tiergarten, sponsored floats and all — and the political demonstration it was registered as had become a trade fair. It left Berlin after 2006.

Tresor, 13 March 1991

In early 1991 Hegemann, Achim Kohlberger and Johnnie Stieler found the safe-deposit vault of the bombed-out Wertheim department store at Leipziger Straße 126a, a few hundred metres from where the Wall had stood, untouched since the war. Tresor opened at midnight on 13 March 1991 behind the vault's steel bars; the record label followed in October, and its first releases were Underground Resistance's X-101 project, then Jeff Mills, Blake Baxter, Juan Atkins and Eddie Fowlkes — the Detroit–Berlin alliance that defined the 90s. The original closed in 2005 for the redevelopment of Potsdamer Platz; Tresor reopened in 2007 in the Kraftwerk Mitte power station on Köpenicker Straße, bars and all.

The 90s city

Planet (1991–93), UFO's direct successor, became E-Werk (1993–97), a substation on Wilhelmstraße that was Berlin's big room, home to Paul van Dyk and the harder, faster, trance-adjacent "Berlin sound" of Westbam's Low Spirit and Marusha. Bunker (1992–96) in a Nazi air-raid shelter was the hardcore and gabber room. WMF, Walfisch, the first Mayday (14 December 1991), Hard Wax — the record shop Mark Ernestus opened in Kreuzberg in 1989 that is still the city's centre of gravity. Nothing was licensed; everything was Zwischennutzung, "interim use", until the owners came back.

Basic Channel, 1993

Ernestus and Moritz von Oswald's Basic Channel records — nine 12"s, no information on the sleeves — invented dub techno: a chord into a delay into a filter, pressed on vinyl at their own Dubplates & Mastering. Chain Reaction, Rhythm & Sound, Maurizio and the whole hypnotic lineage descend from them. It is the other Berlin sound: the one that is not hard.

1998: the bridge

By the late 90s Mitte's scene had been gentrified away and Love Parade was a corporate festival. Norbert Thormann and Michael Teufele's Snax fetish party found a permanent home in a railway depot in Friedrichshain and opened as Ostgut in 1998 — a gay club that took techno seriously, with Panorama Bar upstairs from 2000. It closed on 6 January 2003 after a 30-hour party, and the team went looking for a bigger building (chapter 06).

Techno · 06 · 1988–

Europe beyond Berlin — and the world

Every European city got its own techno in the 90s, usually by taking one Detroit idea and pushing it to an extreme: Frankfurt took the euphoria, Ghent the hoover, Rotterdam the tempo, Cologne the pop, Paris the marathon, Stockholm the loop.

Frankfurt: Sven and the trance line

Sven Väth DJ'd at the airport's Dorian Gray from the early 80s, opened Omen on Junghofstraße in 1988 (it closed in 1998), and with Eye Q (1991) and Harthouse (1992) built the Frankfurt sound: harder than Detroit, more melodic than Berlin, the root of trance. His Cocoon parties (1996) and label (2000) later made him techno's elder statesman. Chris Liebing's CLR and the schranz years (roughly 2000–05: 145 BPM, distorted, loop-based) are Frankfurt's harder second act.

Belgium: new beat to R&S

Belgium's new beat (1987–89) was EBM records played at 33 rpm with the pitch up: slow, sleazy, the hinge between industrial and house. Renaat Vandepapeliere's R&S Records in Ghent then released the records that defined rave: Joey Beltram's "Energy Flash" (1990) and "Mentasm" (with Mundo Muzique, 1991), which invented the hoover; CJ Bolland; Aphex Twin's Selected Ambient Works 85–92. Fuse in Brussels (1994) is still the country's temple, and the 2010s' biggest techno names — Charlotte de Witte, Amelie Lens — are Belgian.

The Netherlands: fast and festival

Rotterdam answered with gabber — Parkzicht, Rotterdam Records (1992), Paul Elstak, 180 BPM distorted kicks — while Amsterdam built the festival industry: Awakenings (from 30 March 1997 at the Gashouder), ADE (1996), and later Dekmantel (festival from 2013). Speedy J's "Pullover" (1991, on Plus 8) made him the Dutch Detroit link; Miss Djax's Djax-Up-Beats in Eindhoven (1989) was releasing Chicago acid before most of Europe had heard it.

Cologne: Kompakt

Wolfgang Voigt, Michael Mayer and Jürgen Paape's shop-turned-label (1998) made minimal gentle, melodic and almost pop: the Total compilations, the Pop Ambient series, Voigt's GAS. Cologne is where techno learned to be pretty without apologising.

France: Garnier and the marathon

Laurent Garnier was a Haçienda resident in 1987–88 before taking house and techno to Paris: Wake Up at Rex Club (1992–), F Communications (1994, with Eric Morand), "Crispy Bacon", and the seven-hour set as an art form. Daft Punk's first records came out on Glasgow's Soma (1994). The free-party tradition — Spiral Tribe's exile after Castlemorton started the teknival movement in 1993 — lives on in France more than anywhere.

Spain, Italy, Sweden

Sonar started in Barcelona in 1994 as a festival of "advanced music"; Valencia's Ruta del Bakalao had been running a harder, stranger techno scene along the coast road since the late 80s. Italy's techno came out of Riccione's Cocoricò (1989) and Naples — Marco Carola, Gaetano Parisio — and in the 2010s produced the hypnotic school (Donato Dozzy, Neel, Voices From The Lake). Adam Beyer's Drumcode (Stockholm, 1996) with Cari Lekebusch and Joel Mull made the Swedish loop techno that became, twenty years later, the biggest brand in the music.

Further out

Tokyo's Liquid Room (1994) hosted Jeff Mills's 1995 recording; Ken Ishii, Fumiya Tanaka and Womb (2000) followed. Zürich's Street Parade (1992) outlasted Love Parade and had its techno culture listed as Swiss heritage in 2017. Tbilisi's Bassiani (2014) and Khidi (2016) made Georgia the 2010s' most consequential new scene: when police raided Bassiani in May 2018, the crowd danced in front of parliament until the interior minister apologised.

Techno · 07 · 2000–2009

The 2000s: minimal, the laptop, and a power station

The decade techno got quiet, then got a building. Minimal stripped the music to clicks and a sub; Ableton and Final Scratch replaced the record bag; Berlin, "poor but sexy", became the place every producer moved to; and in December 2004 a club opened in a Friedrichshain power plant.

Minimal

Robert Hood had named it in 1994, but minimal as a scene began with Richie Hawtin's Decks, EFX & 909 (1999) and DE9: Closer to the Edit (2001), a mix of hundreds of loops cut from records and reassembled in software — the first DJ set that could only exist on a computer. Hawtin moved to Berlin in 2003; Ricardo Villalobos's Alcachofa (2003, Playhouse) and Perlon's catalogue made the Berlin–Frankfurt axis the centre of a music that was slow (124–128), swung, sparse, and lasted all night at Bar25 (2004–10), Club der Visionäre, Watergate (2002) and Panorama Bar. Kompakt in Cologne gave it melody; by 2007 "mnml" was a punchline and a Beatport category.

The laptop

Ableton Live (Berlin, 2001 — Robert Henke and Gerhard Behles of Monolake) and Native Instruments' Traktor (2000) changed what a DJ was. Loops, edits, effects and live resampling (Anatomy, chapter 07) became part of the set, not just the studio; a generation of producers became DJs and vice versa. The vinyl purists kept the Birmingham and Detroit flame; the laptop generation built minimal and, later, the live-techno acts.

Hard, briefly

At the other extreme, schranz — Chris Liebing, Frankfurt, 2000–05 — ran distorted loops at 145 BPM, and Ben Sims's hardgroove kept the funk in it. Both were unfashionable by 2006 and both came back in 2021 as the hard-techno revival's source material.

Berghain, 2004

After Ostgut closed in January 2003, Thormann and Teufele leased a disused combined heat-and-power station on the Kreuzberg–Friedrichshain border — hence the name — with 18-metre ceilings and the concrete left alone. Panorama Bar opened in October 2004, the main floor that December, with one of Funktion-One's largest club installations. Klubnacht ran from Saturday midnight to Monday morning. The residents — Marcel Dettmann, Ben Klock, Cassy, Nick Höppner, Prosumer, Steffi — got their own label, Ostgut Ton, in 2005, and its sound (chapter 08) became the decade's.

Berlin, poor but sexy

Mayor Klaus Wowereit's 2003 phrase described a city with cheap rent, cheap flights, a 24-hour licence culture and more empty buildings than it could use. Producers from everywhere moved there; Resident Advisor, founded in Sydney in 2001, became the scene's newspaper; the Berlin weekend became a tourist product. The clubs' response was the door policy: no phones, no photos, and the selection at Berghain that made Sven Marquardt famous.

Elsewhere

Detroit got its festival (DEMF, 2000; Movement from 2003; Paxahau from 2006). London got fabric's mix series (2001–) and, at the decade's end, dubstep's collision with techno (Shackleton, 2562, Scuba's Hotflush) that produced the 2010s' UK sound. Ibiza got Cocoon (chapter 07).

Techno · 08 · 2000–

Ibiza: techno's summer residency

For its first fifteen years the island had nothing to do with techno. Its own story — Pacha, Ku, Alfredo's Balearic nights at Amnesia, the 1987 trip that lit acid house in London — is house history, and it is in that strand. Techno arrived on a Monday in the summer of 2000, and has spent a quarter of a century deciding whether it belongs there.

Cocoon, 2000

Sven Väth brought Frankfurt's Cocoon to Amnesia for a Monday residency in 2000 and kept it there for nearly two decades. It was the first techno night on the island that lasted: Villalobos, Loco Dice and the Cocoon roster on the Terrace, Väth's closing-night marathons under the ice cannons, trance at sunrise and techno all night, and a label (Cocoon Recordings, 2000) that documented it. The super-clubs had booked techno DJs before; Cocoon made techno a season.

Carl Cox at Space, 2001–2016

Pepe Roselló's Space in Playa d'en Bossa — the morning club with the Terrace under the flight path — gave Carl Cox a Tuesday residency in 2001. For fifteen summers it was the biggest techno room on the island, with Cox moving between house and techno as he always had and his Intec label on the bill. The residency ended on 20 September 2016 with a nine-plus-hour vinyl set — Jeff Mills's "The Bells" in it — and the club closed two weeks later; Hï stands on the site.

ENTER. and the minimal years, 2012–15

Richie Hawtin's ENTER. took over Space's Thursdays for four summers with the M_nus roster, sake, and a production budget that treated the club as an installation: minimal's laboratory on a resort island. Marco Carola's Music On at Pacha (Fridays from 2012) ran the tech-house boundary at the same time — the point where this strand hands over to the house one.

The 2020s

The Belgian wave and the hard-techno names now headline the super-clubs that would not book techno in the 90s; Amnesia's Pyramid and Hï's club room run it weekly. Techno's relationship with the island is still uneasy — the €60 door and the €15 water are the economics Berlin defines itself against — but the open-air long set, the residency as an institution and the eight-hour journey through tempos all owe something to the Terrace.

Techno · 09 · 2010–2019

The 2010s: the Berghain decade

One club's residents defined the sound, one website broadcast it, one festival curated it, and by the end of the decade techno was headlining 50,000-capacity main stages again. Then the revivals began: industrial, hypnotic, electro, and the fight over who the music belongs to.

Ostgut Ton and the rumble

Ben Klock's One (2009) and Berghain 04 mix (2010), Marcel Dettmann's Dettmann (2010), Function, Norman Nodge, Len Faki's Figure and Klockworks: the "Berghain sound" was mid-tempo (128–132), tool-like, built around the kick's reverb tail — the rumble of Anatomy chapter 04 — and designed for ten-hour sets rather than 12" singles. Its restraint became the global default; its imitators became a cliché; the room itself remained the measure of everything.

Boiler Room and Resident Advisor

Blaise Bellville pointed a webcam at a DJ in a Dalston warehouse in 2010, and within three years the crowd standing behind the decks was the image of the decade. Boiler Room, RA's podcasts and Dekmantel's recordings (festival from 2013) meant a set in Amsterdam or Tbilisi was heard everywhere by Monday, and DJs became global brands — Nina Kraviz, Helena Hauff, Amelie Lens, Charlotte de Witte, Peggy Gou — without a hit record.

The revivals

Industrial: Regis's Downwards resurgence, Ancient Methods, Perc Trax, Paula Temple, Dominick Fernow's Vatican Shadow and Hospital Productions, and the French school (Dax J, I Hate Models) — Birmingham's line returned at 140. Hypnotic: Donato Dozzy, Rrose, Voices From The Lake (2012), the Italian and Bunker New York circuit, dub techno's grandchildren. UK drum machine: Blawan and Pariah as Karenn (2011), Objekt, Surgeon's modular sets, Hessle Audio's techno turn. Electro: Helena Hauff, DJ Stingray, Drexciya's reissues.

Festival techno

Drumcode's Adam Beyer became the biggest DJ in techno by making it a main-stage music again: offbeat bass, big claps, rolling arpeggios at 135. Awakenings, Time Warp, Printworks (2017–23) and the Belgian wave filled rooms the Berghain generation considered the enemy. By 2019 the two camps — "underground" and "business techno" — were the scene's main argument.

Politics

fabric was closed by Islington council in September 2016 after two drug deaths and reopened in January 2017 after a campaign that reached parliament; Bassiani was raided in May 2018 and Tbilisi danced in front of the parliament building; Berlin's clubs fought the "Clubsterben" of rent rises and began the lobbying that produced the 2024 heritage listing. And from Detroit outward, a conversation grew about techno as Black music — UR's "Electronic Warfare", Honey Dijon, DeForrest Brown Jr's Assembling a Black Counter Culture (2022) — and about who was booked, paid and credited in a scene whose origin it was.

Techno · 10 · 2020–

The 2020s: harder, faster, everywhere

The clubs shut in March 2020 and reopened to a generation that had learned techno from phones. The result was the fastest, loudest version of the music since 1992 — and, underneath it, every older dialect still running.

The pause

Europe's clubs closed within a week in March 2020. Berghain became an art gallery (Studio Berlin, 2020); Awakenings and Boiler Room streamed to empty rooms; Berlin's Clubcommission counted the venues that would not reopen. When the doors opened again in 2021–22 the queues were longer and younger than ever.

Hard techno

What the new crowd wanted was speed: 150–160 BPM, distorted kicks, trance leads, hoovers and snare rolls, the sum of 2000s schranz, 90s hardcore and Dutch hardstyle. 999999999 (Italy), I Hate Models (France), Trym, Sara Landry, Kobosil's R Label, Verknipt's Dutch raves and a TikTok-scale audience made "hard techno" a separate genre with its own festivals, and Charlotte de Witte and Amelie Lens the biggest headliners in dance music. The old guard called it hardstyle with better marketing; the new guard didn't ask.

The counter-currents

Hardgroove came back (Ben Sims, Reelow — Detroit swing at 140); trance came back; the hypnotic and dub lineages kept their rooms; electro and EBM filled the Panorama Bar morning. Tresor turned thirty in 2021, Jeff Mills toured the Liquid Room set for its thirtieth anniversary in 2025–26, and Movement, Dekmantel and Houghton kept the canon. Techno in 2026 is not one music but seven or eight running in parallel at different tempos, which is what the Mixer at the end of this guide is for.

Heritage and loss

On 13 March 2024 "Techno Culture in Berlin" was added to Germany's national inventory of intangible cultural heritage under the UNESCO convention, after a campaign begun by Rave The Planet and Dr. Motte — a recognition sought as protection against the city's development pressure. Nine months later Watergate closed (31 December 2024), and Renate followed in 2025. London lost Printworks in 2023 and gained Drumsheds; Fold (2018) runs the Berlin model in Canning Town. The music is bigger than it has ever been; the rooms are harder to keep.

Techno · 12

Clubs

Techno is a music made for specific rooms, and the rooms shaped it back. A composite floor plan first, then the rooms that mattered.

Anatomy of a techno club: a composite of Berghain, Tresor, Space and fabric. The sound system is the architecture; everything else is removed.

Sound

Berghain's main floor opened in 2004 with one of Funktion-One's largest installations, replaced in 2023; Panorama Bar got a line-array system in 2017. fabric's Room One has a "bodysonic" floor with transducers under the boards. The rule everywhere: subs on the ground, no reflections, a sound engineer on duty all night.

Time

The Music Institute ran midnight to 9am; Space opened at 8am; Berghain runs from Saturday midnight to Monday morning. Techno's form — long, tool-like, cumulative — follows from sets of six to ten hours in rooms with no clocks.

Door

Selection, no photos, no guest lists: the Berlin model is the opposite of the Ibiza one, and the reason the music inside can be uncompromising. Shoom's "members only" and the Music Institute's cards were the same idea thirty years earlier.

Festivals and events

From a truck on the Ku'damm to 40,000 in a Mannheim exhibition hall: the events that turned a club music into a calendar.

The techno year, by month. Distance from the centre is only to spread the labels.

Three kinds of event

The parade — Love Parade, Street Parade, Rave The Planet — is techno as public demonstration, which is literally how Love Parade was registered in 1989. The indoor rave — Mayday, Time Warp, Awakenings in the Gashouder — is the German-Dutch industry of production, lasers and sound, descended from the 90s hall parties. The curated festival — Sonar, Dekmantel, Movement, Draaimolen, Houghton — is where the canon is set and the next decade's DJs are booked first.

Two that ended

Castlemorton (1992) ended Britain's free-party era by provoking the Criminal Justice Act. Love Parade ended on 24 July 2010 in Duisburg, when a crush in the single tunnel entrance killed 21 people; the organisers cancelled the event permanently.

And one that isn't a festival

Boiler Room (2010) is the event the 2010s invented: a set broadcast from a room, with the crowd behind the DJ. It did more for the geography of techno — Tbilisi, Lisbon, Johannesburg, Shanghai — than any festival.

Rave · 00

What a rave is

Rave is not a genre. It is a way of gathering — all night, outside the licensed room, to music that is faster than it should be — that Britain invented twice: once in the jazz cellars of 1950s Soho, and once in the fields around the M25 in 1988. This strand is the second time, from the acid house parties to jungle and what came after.

The word

"Rave" is late Middle English, from the Old French raver: to show signs of madness, to talk wildly; by the 18th century to be in high spirits; by the 20th, to praise. In late-1950s London it meant a party: the jazz musician Mick Mulligan was "King of the Ravers", Buddy Holly sang "Rave On" (1958), and the beatniks of Soho held all-night "raves" in cellars on Gerrard Street and Windmill Street and on river-boat shuffles up the Thames — socially triumphant, financially hopeless, as George Melly wrote. The Yardbirds' American album was Having a Rave Up (1965). Then the word went quiet for twenty years.

The second meaning

It came back in 1988 attached to acid house, and by 1989 meant something specific: a party of thousands in a warehouse, a field or an aircraft hangar, unlicensed, found by phone line and pirate radio, running until the sun came up. By 1991 "rave" also named a music — hardcore — and by 1994 an Act of Parliament. By 1996 it was a word teenagers used for a licensed night at a leisure centre, and by 2006 "nu-rave" was a fashion revival. The strand follows the word through all of that.

What rave took from each neighbour

  • From house (its own strand): the records of 1987–88, the Ibiza trip, the ecstasy, the smiley, Shoom's "members only" and the Haçienda's crowd.
  • From techno (its own strand): the Belgian and Detroit records of 1990–91, the 909, the harder tempos — and, in 1992, a parting of the ways.
  • Its own: the breakbeat, the piano, the MC, the sampler, the pirate station, the sound system, the convoy, the free party and the fight with the state.

Rave · 01 · 1988–1990

Acid house parties and the orbital raves

The clubs filled in a year. When Shoom's 300 became Spectrum's 1,500 and the Haçienda's queue went round the block, the promoters went looking for bigger rooms, and found them outside the law: warehouses in Hackney, Blackburn and Jenkins Lane, then airfields and fields around the M25.

Warehouses, 1988

Genesis (Wayne Anthony) and Sunrise (Tony Colston-Hayter) ran East London warehouse parties through 1988 while the clubs were still the story. Colston-Hayter — a 22-year-old with a mobile phone and a gift for publicity, soon "the Acid House King" — understood that the party could be the brand: Sunrise's events moved from warehouses to Santa Pod raceway and White Waltham airfield, and the information moved with them, through phone lines, pirate stations and service-station meeting points. The ecstasy death of Janet Mayes in October 1988 ended the press's honeymoon; the police raids on warehouse parties began the same month. At one in Sevenoaks a 20-year-old called Paul Hartnoll was beaten by uniformed officers. He went home and formed Orbital.

The orbital summer, 1989

Sunrise 5000 at White Waltham on 24 June 1989 — eleven thousand people in an aircraft hangar — gave the Sun its "Spaced Out!" front page and the government a cause. Energy (Jeremy Taylor and Karl Jewell), Biology (brought south by Coventry's Amnesia House), Raindance, World Dance at Lydd airport and dozens of others ringed the motorway so that convoys of cars could reach them in under an hour. The cat-and-mouse with the police — a gathering point, a phone number, a last-minute location, roadblocks, a party moved three times in a night — became the folklore. Thames Valley Police formed the Pay Party Unit; Graham Bright's private member's bill, the Entertainments (Increased Penalties) Act 1990, raised fines to £20,000 and six months, and the summer of 1990 was quieter.

The North

Blackburn, 1989–90: Paul Taylor's crew and others ran warehouse parties for thousands every weekend in the Lancashire mill towns until a police operation in February 1990 broke it with mass arrests. Shelley's in Stoke, Quadrant Park in Bootle and Coventry's Eclipse — Britain's first legal all-night club, 1990 — were the licensed answers; the Haçienda was already a rave by 1989 and would pay for it.

What was played

Chicago acid and house, Detroit, Belgian new beat and the first R&S records, Italian piano house, Balearic oddities, and from 1989 the British records made for these rooms: "Voodoo Ray", "Pacific State", "Chime", "Hardcore Uproar". Carl Cox on three decks, Colin Dale and Colin Faver from Kiss, Graham Gold, Trevor Fung, Mr. C, Fabio and Grooverider playing faster every week.

Rave · 02

Pirate radio

A rave needed a way to tell ten thousand people where to go without telling the police, and a way to hear the records before the shops had them. Tower blocks with a transmitter on the roof did both. London's pirates were the nervous system of the scene for fifteen years, and the reason jungle, garage and grime exist.

How it worked

A transmitter in a lock-up, an aerial on a council block, a studio somewhere else connected by a microwave link, a phone line for shout-outs and a second line for rave information in half-code. The DTI's raids took the aerial, rarely the studio; stations came back the next weekend on a different roof. The DJ's show was the only place to hear white labels, the station's name was on every flyer, and the crew that ran it usually ran a club night too.

The stations

Kiss FM (pirate from 1985, legal from 1990) with Colin Dale, Colin Faver, Norman Jay and Graham Gold was the senior service and the techno connection. Centreforce on 88.3 was the first acid house pirate in 1989, raided weekly, Jumpin' Jack Frost on the decks. Sunrise FM, Fantasy FM, Pulse and Dance FM carried the orbital years; Kool FM (1991) became the jungle station and never stopped; Don, Eruption, Weekend Rush, Unity and Lazer carried hardcore and jungle through the mid-90s; Rinse (1994) took garage and grime into the 2000s and got a licence in 2010. When the authorities eventually legalised Kiss and Rinse it was an admission that the pirates had been the broadcasters that mattered.

Why it matters to the music

Pirate radio is why British dance music moves in dialects. A station's crew, its MCs and its listeners' phone-ins were a feedback loop that could turn a sound into a scene in six months — hardcore into jungle on Kool, garage into grime on Rinse — faster than any label. It is also why so much of the music has an MC on it: the voice was there before the record.

Rave · 03 · 1990–1992

Hardcore

Rave's own music. Faster than house, built from samples rather than synths, with a breakbeat where the kick should be and a piano where the chord should be: for two years the biggest-selling dance music in Britain, made in bedrooms and sold from the boot of a car.

Ingredients

An Akai sampler (the S950 was the standard), a breakbeat lifted from a funk record ("Amen", "Think", "Funky Drummer"), a sub-bass from reggae, a hoover or a stab from a Belgian record, a piano riff from Italian house, a vocal snatch from anywhere, and a tempo that went from 125 in 1990 to 140 by 1991 and 150 by 1992. Shut Up and Dance in Hackney had been doing it since 1989 as hip-hop at the wrong speed; by 1991 it had a name.

The records

"Energy Flash" and "Mentasm" from Ghent, "Dominator" and "Anasthasia" from the Low Countries; then the British wave — The Prodigy's "Charly" (a public-information cat at 140), Altern 8 in chemical suits with "Activ 8", SL2's "On a Ragga Tip", Shut Up and Dance's "Raving I'm Raving" at No. 2 and withdrawn within a week, Urban Shakedown's "Some Justice", Acen, 2 Bad Mice, the Rat Pack, Liquid's "Sweet Harmony". The labels were shops and bedrooms: Suburban Base out of Boogie Times in Romford, Moving Shadow, Reinforced, Production House, Kickin'. The charts noticed; the music papers sneered; the kids didn't care.

The rooms

Raindance went licensed first; Fantazia put 25,000 in a Gloucestershire stately home and sold the videos; Dreamscape and Helter Skelter made the Sanctuary in Milton Keynes the hardcore cathedral; World Dance ran Lydd airport; the Eclipse in Coventry and Shelley's in Stoke ran it as clubs. Every event sold a tape pack — six cassettes of the night's sets with the MCs on — and the tape pack, not the record, is how hardcore was actually consumed.

The DJs and MCs

Slipmatt, Ellis Dee, Ratpack, Top Buzz, Mickey Finn, DJ Hype, Jumpin' Jack Frost, Carl Cox, Fabio and Grooverider, Doc Scott, LTJ Bukem, Grooverider's younger rivals on Kool. The MC — Ratpack's Evenson Allen, Top Buzz's Mad P, MC Conrad — was hardcore's addition to the DJ booth, and jungle's inheritance.

Rave sped up 35 BPM in five years, then split: jungle kept accelerating, happy hardcore stayed at 165, and the straight-beat rooms went back to 135.

Rave · 04 · 1990–1994

The free party and the protest

While the promoters went licensed, another part of the scene went the other way: sound systems in trucks, parties with no door at all, and an alliance with the travellers, squatters and road protesters who had been fighting the state since the Battle of the Beanfield.

Lineage

Britain's free festivals — Windsor, Stonehenge, the Peace Convoy — predated acid house by fifteen years, and in June 1985 Wiltshire police had smashed the convoy's vehicles at the Battle of the Beanfield. When the orbital raves were priced and policed out of existence in 1990, the sound systems that replaced them inherited the travellers' sites, their ethics and their enemies.

Spiral Tribe

Founded in West London in 1990 by Mark Harrison, Debbie Griffith, Simone and friends, Spiral Tribe were a sound system, a crew and a creed: "make some noise", no door price, the rig running until the police arrived and often after. Their music was techno — hard, fast, acid-driven — and their parties were in squats, disused airfields and common land. Mutoid Waste Company built the sculpture and the vehicles; Bedlam, Circus Warp, DiY from Nottingham, Exodus from Luton and Desert Storm (who later took their rig to besieged Sarajevo) were the other systems. In May 1992 they all converged on Castlemorton Common.

After Castlemorton

The Tribe were charged with public-order offences and acquitted after a four-month trial that cost the state £4 million; by then they had left for France, where their parties became the teknival — the free festival as a European movement that still runs. In London the sound went into Stay Up Forever's acid techno; in the protest movement it went into Reclaim the Streets (1995), the M11 and Newbury road camps, and the carnivals against capitalism. The free party is the one part of rave that never went legal and never stopped.

Rave · 05 · 1989–1994

The Criminal Justice Act

No other music has a law written against it by name. The Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994 was the end of a five-year escalation from tabloid panic to statute, and the point at which rave either went indoors or went abroad.

1989: the panic

The Sun's "Evil of Ecstasy" and "Spaced Out!" front pages, Sunrise 5000, questions in the Commons, and Margaret Thatcher's personal interest — she was briefed on acid house parties in the summer of 1989 and asked what the police were doing. Thames Valley's Pay Party Unit coordinated intelligence across forces; roadblocks and helicopter surveillance followed; promoters were prosecuted under licensing and public-order law that had never been designed for a party in a field.

1990: the Bright Bill

Graham Bright MP's Entertainments (Increased Penalties) Act raised the maximum fine for an unlicensed party from £2,000 to £20,000 and added six months in prison. It worked on the commercial promoters, who went licensed, and not at all on the sound systems, who had never charged.

1994: the Act

Castlemorton (chapter 06 of this strand) made the free festival a national emergency. The Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994 — Michael Howard's omnibus bill — gave police the power to stop a "rave" of 100 or more people in the open air, to direct people away from it, to seize equipment and to arrest anyone returning within a week; it defined the music as sounds "wholly or predominantly characterised by the emission of a succession of repetitive beats", criminalised aggravated trespass, and removed the right to silence for good measure. It also targeted travellers, squatters and hunt saboteurs — the whole alliance of chapter 04 — which is why the alliance marched together against it: three demonstrations in London in 1994, the last ending in a riot in Hyde Park.

What it did

It ended the free party as a mass movement in England and sent Spiral Tribe to France. It made the licensed promoter, the superclub and the festival the legal shape of British dance music for a generation. And it gave the music a political identity it has never quite lost: Autechre's "Flutter" was written so that no bar repeated; Orbital, the Prodigy and Dreadzone recorded against the bill; the sound systems that stayed went underground and are still there.

Rave · 06 · 1990–1999

Licensed raves and the superclubs

The rave came indoors twice: first as the licensed all-nighter — Raindance, Fantazia, Universe — and then as the superclub, which took the rave's scale and gave it a bar, a brand and a dress code.

The licensed rave

Raindance, from a Jenkins Lane warehouse in 1989, was the first promoter to go to the council, the fire officer and the police and come back with a licence; the model — a leisure centre or exhibition hall, security, first aid, a 6am close, a top-tier line-up across hardcore, techno and house — was copied by Fantazia (25,000 at Castle Donington in 1992), Dreamscape and Helter Skelter at the Sanctuary, World Dance, Universe's Tribal Gathering (Lower Pertwood 1993; Kraftwerk 1997), and the Eclipse. It preserved the scale and killed the risk, and the tape pack and the flyer became an industry.

The superclub

Ministry of Sound (September 1991, Elephant and Castle) was built around a sound system, Garage-style, with no alcohol licence at first; Cream (Liverpool, 1992) grew from a club night into a brand and a festival; Gatecrasher in Sheffield, Renaissance, Back to Basics in Leeds, Trade's Sunday mornings at Turnmills, The Fridge in Brixton with Love Muscle and Escape from Samsara, The End (1995), fabric (1999). By 1997 "superclub" meant compilations, radio shows, Ibiza residencies and a dress code, and the music inside was house and trance, not hardcore. Rave as an outlaw form had become the British leisure industry.

What was lost and kept

Lost: the field, the convoy, the free door, the sense that it could all be stopped. Kept: the scale, the all-night form, the MC and the pirate station (in jungle), the DJ as headliner, and the festival — Creamfields (1998), Homelands, Global Gathering and eventually every green field in August descend from Tribal Gathering and Fantazia, not from Glastonbury.

Rave · 07 · 1993–

The split: jungle, happy hardcore and after

By 1993 hardcore had become three musics. The breakbeats got faster and darker and became jungle; the pianos and vocals got faster and brighter and became happy hardcore; and the straight 4/4 left for the techno strand. Each kept a piece of the rave.

Jungle, 1993–

At Rage, Fabio and Grooverider pitched breakbeat records to +8 and mixed them over reggae bass; Lennie De Ice's "We Are I.E." (1991), Metalheads' "Terminator" (1992, Goldie and 4hero: timestretch), A Guy Called Gerald's "28 Gun Bad Boy", Origin Unknown's "Valley of the Shadows" and Deep Blue's "The Helicopter Tune" made it a music; Kool FM, AWOL at the Paradise Club and the Blue Note's Metalheadz Sundays made it a scene. Jungle was Black British music in a way hardcore hadn't quite been, and when Goldie's Timeless (1995) and LTJ Bukem's Logical Progression took it to albums it became drum & bass — a genre with its own strand to come.

Happy hardcore, 1993–

Slipmatt, Vibes, Dougal and Hixxy kept the 4/4 kick under the breakbeat, sped the piano and vocal records to 165–175 and turned hardcore into a children's crusade: Helter Skelter, Dreamscape, United Dance, the tape packs, the whistles. Despised by the press, enormous in the Midlands and the North, and the direct ancestor of UK hardcore, Scottish bouncy techno, donk and — through the Netherlands — happy gabber and hardstyle.

The straight beats

The straight 4/4 end of the rave rooms — Belgian and Dutch records, the harder Detroit — became UK techno's purist strand: Lost, the Orbit, Birmingham, the acid techno of the free parties. That story is told in the techno strand; the crossing page on the split shows both sides.

After

UK garage took the swing and the MCs (1995–2001); big beat took the breaks to the indie disco; nu-rave (2006) took the clothes; the 2010s' "hardcore continuum" argument took the history; and the 2020s' hard techno and the TikTok hardcore revival took the tempo and the hoover back to the field it started in. Rave's afterlives are the rest of British dance music.

Jungle & drum and bass · 00

What jungle is

The one genre Britain invented from scratch. Take hardcore's breakbeat, speed it past 160, chop it until the drummer could never have played it, put reggae bass under it and a Jamaican MC over it: jungle. Slow the vocal down, add a Rhodes and call it drum & bass. Thirty years on it is still the fastest popular music in the world.

The DNA

  • The break: a few bars of a drummer — "Amen, Brother" (The Winstons, 1969), "Think" (Lyn Collins, 1972), "Funky Drummer" — sampled, sliced, re-sequenced and, from 1992, timestretched. Jungle's instrument is a drummer who has been dead for decades.
  • The bass: from reggae sound systems, through hardcore's sub, to the Reese — a detuned sawtooth chord from Kevin Saunderson's 1988 "Just Want Another Chance" that became drum & bass's bass sound.
  • The tempo: 160 in 1993, 170 by 1996, 174 ever since. The half-time trick — the bass moves at 85 while the drums move at 170 — is why it is danceable at all.
  • The MC and the rewind: from the pirates and the dancehall. A record that gets pulled back and played again is the audience's vote.

Where it came from and where it went

From rave (its own strand): the breakbeat hardcore of 1990–92, Rage at Heaven, the pirates, the tape packs. From techno: the Reese, the darkness of techstep, Photek's precision. To house: UK garage took its MCs and bass at a slower tempo. To everywhere: dubstep (2005), footwork's 160 (Chicago), the 2020s revival through Nia Archives and TikTok.

"It's the drums. Nobody else in the world does the drums like this."the standard junglist explanation

Jungle & drum and bass · 01 · 1988–1992

Rage and the breakbeat

Jungle was invented on a Thursday night under Charing Cross station by two DJs who kept pitching the records up. Everything in the genre descends from the four years of Rage.

Rage, 1988–93

Fabio and Grooverider took the Thursday residency at Heaven in 1988 and played the hardest of what the rave scene had: Belgian techno, Detroit, the first Reinforced and Shut Up and Dance records, then the breakbeat hardcore they were partly causing. Their trick was tempo — breakbeat records pitched to +8, the breaks tumbling into each other — and bass: reggae sub under the drums. By 1991 the room was playing something nobody else had a name for; by 1992 the name was arriving from Kingston via the Rebel MC's records and the sound systems' slang — "junglist".

The studios

Shut Up and Dance (Hackney) had been making hip-hop at the wrong speed since 1989. 4hero's "Mr Kirk's Nightmare" (1990) and their Reinforced label (with Goldie from 1992), Rob Playford's Moving Shadow (Stevenage), Suburban Base in Romford, Ibiza Records, Kickin': the labels were record shops and spare rooms within thirty miles of each other. Lennie De Ice's "We Are I.E." (1991) put reggae bass under a break and is usually called the first jungle record; Goldie and 4hero's "Terminator" (1992) timestretched a break for the first time, and the genre had its texture.

The split from hardcore

Through 1992 the breakbeat rooms went faster and darker while the piano rooms went faster and brighter. By the summer of 1993 "hardcore" meant happy hardcore and the breakbeat end had become jungle — told from the rave side in the rave strand's crossing page on the split.

Jungle & drum and bass · 02 · 1993–1995

Jungle

For two years jungle was the most exciting music in the world and the British music press hated it. Kool FM, AWOL, Jungle Fever, the ragga MCs, the Top 10 — and then the fight over what it should become.

The rooms and the radio

Kool FM (1991) was the station; AWOL at the Paradise Club in Islington — Randall, Mickey Finn, Darren Jay, Kenny Ken, Dr S Gachet — the weekly; Jungle Fever, Roast, Thunder & Joy, Telepathy the raves; Blackmarket in Soho, Lucky Spin, Boogie Times the shops. The MCs — Stevie Hyper D, GQ, Moose, Navigator — made the night; the rewind made the record.

Ragga jungle, 1994

M-Beat and General Levy's "Incredible" reached No. 8 in the summer of 1994 and Shy FX and UK Apachi's "Original Nuttah" was everywhere; dancehall vocals over timestretched breaks were jungle's pop moment. The scene's response — a committee of DJs refusing to play Levy after he claimed to "run jungle" — is the first of the genre's many purity fights, and the reason the next phase called itself something else.

The darker rooms

Renegade's "Terrorist" (Ray Keith), Dillinja's bass, Doc Scott's darkcore, Omni Trio's "Renegade Snares" and the Moving Shadow sound, DJ Hype's scratching, DJ Rap as the first woman headliner, Goldie's Metalheadz (1994, with Kemistry and Storm): jungle's other half was heading for Hoxton Square and a Sunday night.

The Amen and the Akai

Jungle is a production method before it is a genre. The break is a sampled drum solo — most often the six seconds from the Winstons' "Amen, Brother" (1969), or "Think" (Lyn Collins), "Funky Drummer" or "Apache" — chopped on an Akai S950 or S1000 into its hits and resequenced at 160 BPM so that no two bars are the same. The bass is a sub sine or a detuned "reese" (from Kevin Saunderson's Reese records) pitched a full octave below the drums. The vocal is a dancehall MC or a soul singer, timestretched: the S1000's trick of slowing a sample without lowering its pitch, which produces the metallic smear first heard on Goldie's "Terminator". The record-anatomy grids on the record pages rebuild the drums and bass; the Anatomy strand's sampling chapter lets you chop your own.

Whose music it was

Jungle was the first British dance music made predominantly by Black British producers and DJs — the sound-system culture of the Caribbean diaspora, speeded up — and the first the press did not know what to do with. The ragga-jungle summer of 1994 put it on Top of the Pops and brought the "jungle is violent" headlines within months; the DJ committee's rejection of General Levy was partly about that and partly about who owned the name. The move to "drum & bass" in 1995 was a rebrand and a retreat; the 2020s revival, with Nia Archives and Sherelle, reclaimed the word.

Jungle & drum and bass · 03 · 1995–2000

Drum & bass

Goldie's Timeless, Bukem's Speed, the Blue Note on a Sunday, Roni Size's Mercury Prize, and the techstep that went the other way: five years in which jungle became drum & bass, got on television and split again.

Atmospheric

LTJ Bukem's "Music" (1993) and Good Looking Records, then Speed at the Mars Bar (1994–96) with Fabio: jazz pads, Rhodes, breaks that rolled rather than hit. "Intelligent" was the press's word and nobody in the scene liked it. Alex Reece's "Pulp Fiction" (1995) invented the two-step break that half the genre still uses; Adam F's "Circles" made the Top 20.

Metalheadz and Timeless

Goldie's Timeless (FFRR, 1995 — "Inner City Life" with Diane Charlemagne, engineered by Rob Playford) was the album that made the music respectable; the Metalheadz Sunday Sessions at the Blue Note in Hoxton (1995–98) were the room where drum & bass found its sound: Photek, Source Direct, Dillinja, Doc Scott, Lemon D, J Majik, Kemistry & Storm. In 1997 Roni Size and Reprazent's New Forms — Bristol, Full Cycle, a double bass — won the Mercury Prize.

Techstep

No U Turn (Nico, Trace, Ed Rush) and then Virus (Ed Rush & Optical, Wormhole, 1998) took the Reese bass and the techno darkness and made techstep: cold, distorted, two-step, Bad Company's "The Nine" (1999) its stadium version. By 2000 the genre had a soulful wing (Fabio named it liquid funk), a dark wing (neurofunk), a jump-up wing (Hype's Ganja Kru, Aphrodite) and a dancefloor centre (RAM, Andy C, "Body Rock").

Bristol

Roni Size, Krust, Die and Suv came out of Bristol's sound-system and Wild Bunch lineage — the same city as Massive Attack and Smith & Mighty — and made Full Cycle and V Recordings the second centre of drum & bass. New Forms (1997), as Reprazent, won the Mercury Prize over Radiohead and the Spice Girls; "Brown Paper Bag" and its double-bass riff are the sound of drum & bass's one summer of consensus. Photek's Modus Operandi the same year is its opposite: austere, Japanese-inflected, drum programming as calligraphy.

The rooms

Speed at the Mars Bar on Thursdays (1994–96, Bukem and Fabio) was where the atmospheric sound was heard first; Metalheadz' Sunday Sessions at the Blue Note in Hoxton Square (1995–98) was the room drum & bass was perfected in, three hundred people and every producer in the genre; Movement at Bar Rumba (Bryan Gee's Thursday, 1997–2005) was the weekly; the Que Club in Birmingham and the Ministry of Sound's "Innovation" were the big rooms; and the rave-sized events — Jungle Fever, One Nation, World Dance — kept the jungle side alive. Kool FM and Rinse were the radio.

Jungle & drum and bass · 04 · 2000–

The 2000s and the revival

Drum & bass never went away; it went to Fabric on Fridays, to Hospital's parties, to Pendulum's stadiums, to Sardinia, and then — in the 2020s — back to the jungle it started as.

The institution

Fabriclive, Hospitality at Brixton Academy, Andy C's residencies and the double-drop, RAM's chart records (Chase & Status, Sub Focus, Wilkinson), Hospital's liquid (High Contrast, London Elektricity, Calibre, Netsky), neurofunk's sound-design arms race (Noisia, Phace), Sun and Bass on a Sardinian beach (2004–), Let It Roll in a Czech airfield. Pendulum's Hold Your Colour (2005) sold half a million and made drum & bass a rock festival act; Chase & Status made it a pop one. By 2010 it was the biggest British genre nobody wrote about.

The revival

The 2010s' jungle revival (Chimpo, Sully, Tim Reaper, Dwarde, the Rupture parties) was a connoisseur's thing until 2021, when Nia Archives's "Headz Gone West" and PinkPantheress's "Break It Off" — Adam F's "Circles" sampled at 20 million streams — put jungle on TikTok and in the charts. Sherelle's Boiler Room at 160, footwork's crossover, the "jungle is massive" T-shirts: the 2020s' fastest music is the 1993 one.

The dialects

After 2000 the genre split into dialects it has kept since. Liquid funk — Fabio's name, Hospital's catalogue, High Contrast, Calibre, London Elektricity — is the soulful side, played on Radio 1 and at Hospitality's Brixton Academy shows. Neurofunk — Virus, Noisia, Phace, Black Sun Empire — is techstep refined into sound design, the bassline as an instrument. Jump-up — Hype's Playaz, Aphrodite, later Hazard and DJ Guv — is the bassline hook and the rewind. Dubstep, born in Croydon around 2002 at 140 BPM, is jungle's half-speed cousin and borrowed its sub and its MCs; drumstep and halftime borrowed back.

The stadiums and the revival

Pendulum's Hold Your Colour (2005) and Chase & Status's No More Idols (2011) put drum & bass in arenas with guitars and rock vocals; Andy C's RAM Records became a major-label-scale operation; Wilkinson, Sub Focus and Rudimental had Top 10 hits. Then, around 2020, the original word came back: Nia Archives' "Headz Gone West" and Forbidden Feelingz, PinkPantheress's "Break It Off" over a jungle break, Sherelle's 160 sets, Tim Reaper's Future Retro, the "jungle" tag on a generation's TikTok. The genre is thirty years old and has never once been unfashionable in Britain for more than a year.

Trance · 00

What trance is

Techno's melodic exit. Trance took the 909 and the 4/4, added the arpeggio, the breakdown and the key change, and for five years around the millennium was the biggest dance music on earth. It then became a religion with a radio show, and in the 2020s its sound came back inside hard techno.

The DNA

  • The arpeggio and the supersaw: a sequence of saw-wave notes, detuned seven ways (the Roland JP-8000's supersaw, 1996), filtered open over sixteen bars.
  • The breakdown: a minute without drums, a melody, a snare roll, the drop. Trance is built around the moment the kick comes back.
  • Tempo: 135–140, straight, with the bass on the offbeat; the kick is techno's but the top is a film score.
  • Euphoria as policy: major keys, suspended chords, the key change at the second drop. The word was Frankfurt's; the feeling was Goa's and Ibiza's.

Lineage

Klaus Schulze and Tangerine Dream's sequencers; The KLF's "Pure Trance" singles (1988); Belgian new beat; acid. Then Frankfurt 1990–93 (its own chapter), the Goa branch (chapter 02), the Dutch-British boom (chapter 03) and the long afterlife (chapter 04).

Trance · 01 · 1990–1995

Frankfurt and Berlin

Trance was named in Frankfurt in 1990 and built there over the next three years by a few producers, one DJ and two labels; Berlin's E-Werk gave it its first star.

Frankfurt

Dance 2 Trance — Dag Lerner and Rolf Ellmer — released "We Came in Peace" in 1990 and the word stuck. Jam & Spoon's "Stella" (1992, on R&S) and their remix of Age of Love's 1990 Belgian record, Hardfloor's three-303 "Acperience 1" (1992), Cosmic Baby, Oliver Lieb's Spicelab, Resistance D: the sound was techno with a melody and a build, played at Sven Väth's Omen and in Dorian Gray's airport basement. Väth's labels — Eye Q (1991: Cygnus X's "The Orange Theme", Energy 52's "Café del Mar") and Harthouse (1992) — gave it a catalogue; Väth's "An Accident in Paradise" (1992) gave it an album.

Berlin

Mark Reeder's MFS label signed Cosmic Baby and a young Paul van Dyk; their Visions of Shiva records (1992) and van Dyk's "For an Angel" (1994) came out of E-Werk's Dubmission nights, where he was resident. Berlin's trance was softer and more pop — Marusha's "Somewhere Over the Rainbow" was its Love Parade anthem — and techno purists in the city never forgave it.

What divided it from techno

Not the drums — the same 909, the same tempo — but the intention. Techno withheld; trance delivered. By 1995 Frankfurt had chosen: Väth kept Cocoon on the techno side, Harthouse went harder (hard trance: Scot Project, later Yoji), and the melodic line left for the Netherlands and Britain.

The tools

Trance's sounds are specific. The supersaw — seven detuned sawtooths in one oscillator, from Roland's JP-8000 of 1996 — is the lead; before it, the Juno and the Alpha Juno, with Jam & Spoon's "Stella" using a sampled string stab. The arpeggio is a 16th-note sequence on a minor triad; the bass is on the offbeat, pumping against the kick; the breakdown is a minute of pads and a filter opening into the drop, which in a trance record is the structural point of the whole thing. The Mixer's "Trance 1993" preset is the Frankfurt sound, "Trance 1999" the one it became; the record-anatomy grids for "Stella", "The Age of Love" and "Café del Mar" show the form.

Dorian Gray, Omen, Harthouse

Frankfurt had the infrastructure trance needed: Dorian Gray (1978–2000), the club in the airport's basement, open until Monday morning because the airport's licensing allowed it, where Väth, Talla 2XLC and DJ Dag played; the Omen (1988–98), Väth's own room on Junghofstrasse; Delirium, the record shop; and the Eye Q / Harthouse / Recycle Or Die labels run out of one office with Heinz Roth and Matthias Hoffmann. Harthouse's harder end — Hardfloor, Arpeggiators, Resistance D — became hard trance and then, via Scot Project and later Yoji Biomehanika, a scene of its own; the melodic end went to Berlin's MFS and then to the Netherlands.

Trance · 02 · 1970s–

Goa and psytrance

The other trance: born on a beach in India from hippie parties, acid house cassettes and one American DJ, and still running its own world of festivals, fractals and full moons.

Anjuna

Goa had been a hippie destination since the 1960s; Goa Gil — Gilbert Levey, from San Francisco — arrived in 1969 and by the late 80s was playing all-night parties on Anjuna beach from cassettes of European electronic music: EBM, acid, the stranger end of new beat and Frankfurt. The parties were rituals, the music mixed on tape decks, the tourists took the cassettes home.

London, 1993

Youth's Dragonfly label (1993) and Raja Ram's TIP (1994) put the Goa sound on vinyl: Hallucinogen's "LSD" (Simon Posford, 1995) is its masterpiece, Juno Reactor its film-score wing, Astral Projection and Infected Mushroom (Israel) its biggest acts. Return to the Source at the Fridge (1993–) was the London party; Paul Oakenfold's 1994 Radio 1 "Goa Mix" took it to a million listeners and into his Perfecto and Cream sets, which is how Goa fed the trance boom of chapter 03.

Psytrance

By 1997 "Goa" had become "psytrance": faster (145+), darker, more precise, with its own festivals — Boom in Portugal (1997–), Ozora, Universo Paralello — and a global network that has never needed the rest of dance music. Shpongle (Posford and Raja Ram) is its chill-out; Vini Vici's crossover (2015–) its brief pop moment.

Goa Gil and the full moon

Gilbert Levey, a San Francisco hippie who arrived in Goa in 1970, was the DJ of the Anjuna beach parties for fifty years: first with rock and reggae on cassette, then — after the tapes from London's acid summer reached the beach in 1988 — with electronic music, played off DAT through generators at full-moon parties that ran from dusk to noon. He called the all-night party a "redefinition of the ancient tribal ritual for the 21st century" and never released a record; the DJs and producers who followed him home — Juno Reactor, Hallucinogen, Man With No Name, Astral Projection in Israel — made Goa trance a genre in 1993–95. Goa's own parties were banned by noise laws in 2000; they went to Boom in Portugal, Ozora in Hungary, and the Israeli and Japanese scenes that kept psytrance alive.

Trance · 03 · 1995–2001

The boom

For about five years trance was the mainstream of dance music: Gatecrasher's cyber-kids, Cream's Oakenfold residency, the Dutch producers, Radio 1, Ibiza anthems, "Sandstorm", and a DJ at the Olympics.

Britain

Robert Miles's "Children" (1995) sold five million and opened the door; BT's "Flaming June" (1997), Chicane's "Offshore", Binary Finary's "1998", Art of Trance's "Madagascar" and the Hooj Choons and Platipus catalogues walked through it. Gatecrasher at the Republic in Sheffield (1996–) and Godskitchen in Birmingham were the rooms; Judge Jules on Radio 1 and Ministry's Trance Nation compilations the broadcast; Paul Oakenfold's Cream residency (1997–99) and Perfecto the brand. Sasha and Digweed ran the progressive wing — Renaissance, Northern Exposure, Twilo in New York — long, building, less sugar.

The Netherlands

Ferry Corsten (System F's "Out of the Blue", Gouryella), Tiësto (Black Hole, the Magik mixes), Armin van Buuren ("Communication", 1999), Rank 1's "Airwave", Trance Energy at the Jaarbeurs, Sensation in white at the ArenA: by 1999 the sound had moved to Holland and acquired its definitive shape — the uplifting breakdown, the supersaw, the 138 BPM. Darude's "Sandstorm" (Finland, 1999) was its biggest single; Tiësto playing the Athens Olympics opening ceremony (2004) its peak.

Ibiza

Cream at Amnesia, Judge Jules at Eden, the "Ibiza anthem" as a summer industry — the island's trance years were the superclub era's commercial high tide, and the moment techno's Cocoon on the Monday looked like a resistance movement.

The records

The boom's records are the ones everyone can hum: Binary Finary's "1998", Paul van Dyk's "For an Angel" (1994, and the 1998 remix), Chicane's "Offshore", Robert Miles's "Children", Energy 52's "Café del Mar" (1993, and the 1997 remixes that made it), Darude's "Sandstorm" (1999, the gated supersaw that became a meme), Rank 1's "Airwave", System F's "Out of the Blue", ATB's "9pm (Till I Come)", and the vocal trance of Delerium's "Silence" (Tiësto's mix) and Oakenfold's "Southern Sun". Judge Jules and Pete Tong on Radio 1 played them on Friday nights; the Ministry of Sound and Gatecrasher compilations sold them by the million.

Cyber-kids and superclubs

Gatecrasher at the Republic in Sheffield (1996–2007) was the boom's room: Scott Bond and Judge Jules, a crowd in fluorescent fun-fur and glowsticks that the press named cyber-kids, a night that started with the Gatecrasher logo cut into the crowd's hair. Cream in Liverpool had Oakenfold's residency (1997–99) and the Cream Ibiza summers at Amnesia; Godskitchen in Birmingham, Slinky in Bournemouth, Passion in Coalville were the regional cathedrals. In the Netherlands, Trance Energy at Utrecht's Jaarbeurs (1999–2012) and Sensation at the Amsterdam ArenA (2000–) made it a stadium music, and Armin van Buuren's A State of Trance radio show (2001–) its institution.

Trance · 04 · 2002–

After the peak

The boom ended around 2002, the way booms do: the sound got formulaic, the press moved on, the big names drifted to EDM. What was left was a devoted global audience, a radio show with a thousand episodes, and a surprising afterlife inside techno.

The institution

Armin van Buuren's A State of Trance (radio from 2001; ASOT 1000 in 2021) and Armada became the genre's church; Above & Beyond's Anjunabeats and Group Therapy its congregation ("Sun & Moon", 2011); Dreamstate (US, 2015–) and Luminosity its festivals. Tiësto left for EDM in 2010; Armin stayed and headlined the main stages anyway. Trance's audience never shrank — it stopped being counted.

The afterlife

Psytrance kept its own world (chapter 02). Hard trance (Scot Project's Frankfurt, Yoji, Kutski) survived in the hard dance scene and returned, in the 2020s, inside hard techno: the hoovers, the supersaw leads, the snare rolls and the breakdowns of 150-BPM techno are trance's furniture, moved into a darker room. Evian Christ's Trance Party, the "trance revival" discourse, Sam Goku and the Berlin crowd: the word that techno once used as an insult is back as a compliment.

What survived

The institution did: ASOT reached its thousandth episode in 2021 with arena shows on four continents; Above & Beyond's Group Therapy and Anjunabeats turned trance into a community with its own vocabulary; Armin, Tiësto and Paul van Dyk kept touring; psytrance kept its own calendar at Boom and Ozora. The sound did, too, in disguise — in the EDM of 2010–15 (Swedish House Mafia, Avicii, Hardwell are trance structures at house tempo), in Charlotte de Witte and Amelie Lens's 2020s techno with its trance breakdowns, in hard techno's hoovers and supersaws, in the "trance revival" the 2020s' younger DJs keep announcing. The techno strand's last chapter and the trance strand's first fork are the same page from two sides.

House · 01 · 1977–1988

Chicago

House was disco's survival strategy. When the backlash came — Disco Demolition Night at Comiskey Park, 12 July 1979 — the music went back underground into the Black and gay clubs it had come from, and in Chicago a DJ from New York rebuilt it with a drum machine.

The Warehouse, 1977–82

Robert Williams brought Frankie Knuckles from New York's Continental Baths to open the Warehouse at 206 South Jefferson Street in 1977. Knuckles played disco classics, Philly soul and European electronic pop, re-edited on reel-to-reel and, from the early 80s, underpinned by a Roland rhythm box bought from Derrick May. The crowd was Black, Latino and gay; the record shops shortened "the music they play at the Warehouse" to house. Knuckles left for the Power Plant in 1982; Ron Hardy took over the city's imagination at the Music Box — faster, rawer, playing tapes four times in a night.

The records, 1984–87

Jesse Saunders's "On and On" (1984) was the first house record pressed; Larry Sherman's Trax and Rocky Jones's DJ International the labels; Marshall Jefferson's "Move Your Body" (1986, the piano), Larry Heard's "Can You Feel It" (deep house), Steve "Silk" Hurley's "Jack Your Body" (UK No. 1, January 1987), Farley "Jackmaster" Funk's "Love Can't Turn Around", Joe Smooth's "Promised Land". The Hot Mix 5 on WBMX took it to the whole city; "jack" was the dance. In 1987 Phuture's "Acid Tracks" (the acid crossing) sent the sound to London and changed everything.

The city underneath

Reagan's recession and the War on Drugs, the decline of the South Side's industry, the AIDS epidemic arriving in the clubs that were the only safe rooms their crowd had: house was made by and for people the decade had written off, and the clubs were sanctuaries before they were anything else.

House · 02 · 1970–

New York and the garage

Chicago made the records; New York had made the room. The Loft, the Paradise Garage and Larry Levan's sound system are where the DJ became the congregation's priest, and "garage" is the vocal, gospel-rooted house that still carries the name.

The Loft and the Garage

David Mancuso's invitation-only parties at the Loft (1970–) set the terms: a private party, a perfect sound system, no alcohol, the DJ as host. The Paradise Garage (84 King Street, 1977–87) scaled it: Larry Levan's residency, Richard Long's system, a Black and gay crowd of thousands, and a musical range — disco, dub, new wave, the first house imports — that "garage" came to mean. Levan died in 1992; the club closed in 1987; its sound system's descendants are in every serious club in this book.

New York house, 1987–

Todd Terry's sample house ("Can You Party"), Strictly Rhythm (1989), Nervous, Masters at Work (Louie Vega and Kenny Dope), Tony Humphries at Zanzibar in New Jersey, Junior Vasquez at the Sound Factory, Frankie Knuckles back in New York with David Morales at Def Mix: vocal, soulful, gospel in its bones. UK garage (house strand chapter 06) took its name and its records at +8.

The Loft, 1970

David Mancuso's first party, Love Saves the Day, was on Valentine's Day 1970 in his loft at 647 Broadway: an invitation, a fruit punch, balloons, a Klipschorn sound system he had tuned for weeks, and a host who did not mix records so much as place them. The Loft set every rule the house room inherited — a private party rather than a bar, the sound system as the point, the DJ as a presence rather than a performer, a crowd that was Black, Latin, gay and mixed when New York's licensed clubs were none of those. Nicky Siano's Gallery (1973) took the method public; Frankie Knuckles and Larry Levan worked there as teenagers, blowing up the balloons.

Levan

Larry Levan was resident at the Paradise Garage from its opening in 1977 to its closing in September 1987, and the club was built around him: a parking garage on King Street, no alcohol, members only, a Richard Long system he adjusted during the night, and a style that could follow Manuel Göttsching's "E2-E4" with the Clash and then Class Action's "Weekend" and make them one record. He produced too — Taana Gardner's "Heartbeat", the Peech Boys' "Don't Make Me Wait" — and his dub-informed mixes are the root of the garage sound: vocal, spacious, bass-heavy, warm. He died in 1992, aged 38. "Garage" in New Jersey meant Tony Humphries at Club Zanzibar in Newark; in Britain, a decade later, it would mean something at 130 BPM with an MC.

The records

New York house arrived a year after Chicago's and sounded different at once: Todd Terry's sample-collage records as Royal House and Black Riot (1988), Arthur Baker's productions, and then the labels — Strictly Rhythm (1989, Mark Finkelstein and Gladys Pizarro), Nervous (1991), Nu Groove, King Street — that put out Masters at Work, Kerri Chandler, Armand Van Helden and Roger Sanchez through the 1990s. Junior Vasquez's Sound Factory (1989–95) was the Garage's successor as a room; David Morales and Frankie Knuckles's Def Mix turned the remix into a career; and the vocal, gospel-rooted "garage house" of Barbara Tucker, Ultra Naté and Kenny Bobien is the strand that UK garage, speed garage and two-step borrowed their name and their a cappellas from.

House · 03 · 1973–1990

Ibiza and the Balearic beat

The island's own story: hippies, Glory's, Pacha, Ku and Amnesia, and the Argentine DJ whose anything-goes terrace sets became a genre.

The clubs

Glory's — a racing stadium turned club in the 1970s, Bowie and Grace Jones at the bar — came first; Pacha opened in Ibiza Town in 1973 (Ricardo Urgell), Es Paradís in San Antonio in 1975, Amnesia in a farmhouse near San Rafael in 1976, and Ku — the biggest club in the world, open to the sky, a pool in the floor — in 1978. None had roofs until the 1990 noise laws. Café del Mar (1980) and later Café Mambo made the San Antonio sunset a programme.

Alfredo and the Balearic beat

Alfredo Fiorito, from Rosario in Argentina, moved to Ibiza in 1976 and from 1984 played Amnesia's terrace from midnight to past dawn with no genre: Chicago house next to the Cure, Italo next to Chris Rea, reggae next to early Detroit, Art of Noise, the Woodentops. With Leo Mas beside him, and Pippi, Cirillo, César de Melero, Jon Sa Trinxa and José Padilla elsewhere on the island, this was the Balearic beat — a DJ culture of eclecticism and the long set rather than a sound. Tape decks and early samplers, cheap flights and a tourist economy made it possible; ecstasy made it famous.

The export

Four British DJs heard it in August 1987 and took it home (the next chapter). Space opened in 1989; the superclub era, Cocoon and techno's arrival (techno strand, chapter 07) came later. The crossing page on Ibiza carries all three strands' versions.

House · 04 · 1987–1990

The Ibiza Four and London

Paul Oakenfold's birthday, August 1987: Danny Rampling, Nicky Holloway and Johnny Walker on the Amnesia terrace with Alfredo. What they brought home became Shoom, Spectrum, the Trip and Future — the house clubs whose overflow is the rave strand.

The clubs

Rampling's Shoom (December 1987, a Southwark fitness centre, Saturday nights, the smiley, 300 people dancing barefoot and the hardest door in London); Oakenfold's Future (Thursdays at Heaven, the Balearic night) and Spectrum (Mondays at Heaven from April 1988, 1,500 people, "Theatre of Madness"); Holloway's the Trip at the Astoria (June 1988, the crowd in the Charing Cross Road fountain) and later the Milk Bar; Walker at Shoom and Love Ranch; Charlie Chester's Flying; Boy's Own's parties and fanzine (Farley, Weatherall, Heller). The music was Alfredo's: house, Balearic rock, "Voodoo Ray", Weatherall's "Loaded".

Thatcher's Britain

The context matters: a youth culture arriving at the end of a decade of unemployment, the miners' strike, the Big Bang in the City, Section 28 and the poll tax; a generation that wanted a room where none of that applied. Acid house was that room for about eighteen months, before it became rave (its own strand) and before the clubs became an industry (chapter 06).

Shoom

Danny and Jenni Rampling opened Shoom on 5 December 1987 in the Fitness Centre, a gym on Southwark Street under the railway, for about 150 friends: Alfredo's records, a smoke machine that made the room invisible, a smiley-face logo borrowed from Watchmen, and a door policy that kept the crowd small until it could not. It moved to the YMCA on Tottenham Court Road and then to Busby's, and it is the room acid house as a British culture starts in — the one where the music, the drug and the word "acieed" fused. Jenni Rampling's door and the Shoom fanzine kept it a secret for about six months.

Spectrum and the Trip

Oakenfold and Ian St Paul's Spectrum opened at Heaven on a Monday in April 1988 to a few hundred people and was full within three weeks: 1,500 in the main room, "Theatre of Madness", a laser and a crowd that had never been to a gay club. Nicky Holloway's the Trip at the Astoria on Charing Cross Road (June 1988) was the one the tabloids found — the crowd dancing in the street at closing time, chanting "acieed" at the police. By the autumn the Sun was selling smiley T-shirts and, a month later, printing "Evil of Ecstasy"; the rave strand's first chapter picks the story up from there, as the parties left the clubs for the M25.

Boy's Own and the writing

Terry Farley, Andrew Weatherall, Steve Mayes and Cymon Eckel's Boy's Own fanzine (1986–92) was a football-casual zine that went to Shoom and became the movement's own voice — funny, snobbish, suspicious of the tabloids and of the ravers alike. Its parties in the Sussex countryside were the chic end of the summer; its label gave the world Underworld and the Chemical Brothers' first records. The other voices were the Face, i-D and later Mixmag; Matthew Collin's Altered State and Simon Reynolds's Energy Flash wrote the histories.

House · 05 · 1986–1992

Madchester and the North

Manchester had house before London did. The Haçienda's Nude night (1986) played Chicago to a working-class crowd, and by 1989 the city had fused it with its guitar bands into something the press called Madchester.

The Haçienda

FAC 51, opened 21 May 1982 by Factory Records and New Order's money, designed by Ben Kelly with its yellow-and-black pillars, and empty for years. Mike Pickering and Graeme Park's Nude (Fridays from 1986) was the first British house night with a crowd; Hot (summer 1988) was the rave. Tony Wilson's club lost money on every night and closed in June 1997, broken by guns and debt; it is flats.

The city

808 State ("Pacific State"), A Guy Called Gerald ("Voodoo Ray"), the Stone Roses and Happy Mondays as dance bands, Eastern Bloc records, the Thunderdome and Konspiracy: Manchester's version was hybrid — DJs and bands on the same bill, baggy jeans and bucket hats — and it spread: Quadrant Park in Bootle, Shelley's in Stoke (Sasha), Back to Basics in Leeds (1991), the Blackburn warehouses (rave strand). The North's house was always closer to rave than London's, and its superclub — Cream, Liverpool, 1992 — came out of it.

808 State and the records

Manchester made its own house as soon as it heard Chicago's. Gerald Simpson's "Voodoo Ray" (1988) and 808 State's "Pacific State" (1989) — Graham Massey, Martin Price and Darren Partington, named for the drum machine — were the city's two anthems, both made in bedrooms, both Top 20; Mike Pickering's T-Coy had made "Cariño" in 1987; A Guy Called Gerald's later jungle and 808 State's Ninety took the sound further than anyone in London did. Factory's own dance signings never matched them, but New Order's "Blue Monday" (1983) had already been the biggest-selling 12-inch in history and paid for the club.

Madchester and the end

By 1989 the Haçienda's crowd was the Stone Roses and the Happy Mondays' crowd — "Fools Gold", "Wrote for Luck", baggy jeans, the Mondays' Shaun Ryder as the music's ambassador to the indie press — and the city's name was on every magazine. The club's drugs trade brought the gangs, the gangs brought guns, and Greater Manchester Police forced a closure in January 1991; it reopened with metal detectors and lost money until it shut for good on 28 June 1997 and was demolished for flats. Sankeys Soap, the Warehouse Project and the city's techno rooms are its grandchildren; the Blackburn warehouse parties, where thousands went every weekend in 1989–90 when the Haçienda shut at two, are the rave strand's.

House · 06 · 1990–

House after 1990

The strand's longest chapter in the fewest words: house became the default dance music of the world, split into a dozen dialects, and kept every one of them.

Dialects

  • Deep house: from Larry Heard through Kerri Chandler, Theo Parrish and Moodymann (Detroit's own house), to the 2010s' revival.
  • US garage: Masters at Work, Def Mix, Body & Soul in New York.
  • UK garage: US garage at +8 with jungle's MCs — speed garage (Double 99's "RIP Groove", 1997), 2-step (Artful Dodger, MJ Cole), Ayia Napa, then grime.
  • French touch: Daft Punk (from Soma to Homework, 1997), Cassius, Stardust's "Music Sounds Better with You" (1998): filtered disco loops.
  • Tech-house: the crossing with techno — Wiggle, Circoloco, Music On, the 2010s' Beatport chart.
  • Progressive: Sasha and Digweed, Leftfield; the border with trance.
  • Hard house: Trade and Tony De Vit; the gay after-hours lineage.
  • The revival: Disclosure (2012), Peggy Gou, Honey Dijon, the Panorama Bar morning, and house as pop again.

Deep house and garage, 1990–

Larry Heard's "Can You Feel It" had drawn deep house's blueprint in 1986: a warm pad, a walking bassline, a 909 played soft. Kerri Chandler in New Jersey, Mood II Swing, Glenn Underground, Ron Trent and Chez Damier's Prescription in Chicago, and in London the Nuphonic and Paper labels kept it; in the 2000s it was Berlin's Dial and Innervisions (Dixon, Âme's "Rej"), and in the 2010s a global default sound that Beatport eventually filed under a dozen names. Garage, the New York vocal line, became speed garage in London in 1997 — Double 99's "RIP Groove", US garage at +8 with a jungle sub — and then two-step: Artful Dodger, MJ Cole, the Dreem Teem on Kiss, Ayia Napa, and by 2001 the pop charts and then grime. The jungle strand's MCs, the house strand's a cappellas and the rave strand's pirates all meet in it.

France, 1995–

Daft Punk's "Da Funk" (1995, on Soma), Motorbass, Dimitri from Paris, Cassius, Étienne de Crécy's Super Discount and Stardust's "Music Sounds Better with You" (1998): French touch was house built from filtered disco loops, sidechained until the whole record breathed, and it made Paris the second house capital for five years. Homework (1997) and Discovery (2001) are the albums; the Rex Club and Laurent Garnier's F Communications were the techno side of the same city.

The 2010s: tech-house takes over

By the mid-2010s the biggest-selling genre on Beatport was tech-house — Jamie Jones's Hot Creations, Solid Grooves, Fisher's "Losing It" (2018), the Elrow circus — a swung, bassline-led house with techno's sound design that the tech-house crossing page argues about from both sides. Disco came back too (Dimitri, Horse Meat Disco, Todd Terje), as did the deep, slow, Balearic line (DJ Harvey). House is now simply the room's default music almost everywhere in the world, which is a kind of victory and a kind of disappearance; the border with techno has never been thinner, and this strand ends at it.

The machines: a route through forty years

Dance music is the history of cheap machines being used for things their makers never intended. This strand follows the equipment — drum machines, synths, samplers, computers, decks, software — from the second-hand 808 to the browser this book runs in, and links each one to the records it made and the people who made them. The timeline below is the map; the five chapters are the route.

Technology · 01 · 1980–1988

The analogue bargain bin

Roland made the TR-808 (1980), the TB-303 (1981) and the TR-909 (1983) for lounge bands and guitarists practising at home. All three failed, were discontinued, and by 1985 sat in Detroit and Chicago pawn shops for a hundred dollars. Dance music begins with teenagers buying the wrong machines.

Why they were cheap

The 808's transistor kick sounded nothing like a drummer; the 303 could not play like a bass guitarist and was miserable to program; the 909's hybrid of analogue drums and sampled cymbals satisfied nobody. Yamaha's DX7 (1983) made them all obsolete overnight with digital FM, and Roland's own Juno-106 and Alpha Juno (1985) undercut everything with cheap polyphony. The failures went second-hand; the kids bought them.

What they became

The 808 became electro and then house's first records — "Planet Rock", "On and On", the Trax catalogue. The 909 became techno's kick and house's clap: Derrick May's, Jeff Mills's, everyone's. The 303, turned up wrong by Phuture in 1986, became acid. The Juno's chorus became deep house; the Alpha Juno's "WhatThe" patch became the hoover; the SH-101 became hardcore's bassline; the little Casio CZ became the reese. Kraftwerk's Minimoog and Juan Atkins's Korg MS-10 are the strand's prehistory. The Technics SL-1200 (1972) was already the DJ's instrument and stayed it for forty years.

The method

A drum machine, a bass box, a synth, a four-track or a cheap mixer, and a step sequencer — the MC-202, or the machines' own — synced by a DIN cable. No computer until the Atari arrived. The Anatomy strand's first chapters are built on exactly this rig; the Mixer's 1986–88 presets are this rig imitated.

Technology · 02 · 1986–1996

The sampler

The Fairlight cost as much as a house and the Emulator as much as a car; the Akai S900 (1986) cost as much as a good synthesiser, and the S1000 (1988) could do a trick no instrument had done before — stretch time. Britain's whole rave decade was made on it.

Voodoo Ray to Terminator

A Guy Called Gerald made "Voodoo Ray" in 1988 on an S950 that ran out of memory halfway through the word "rage"; by 1990 the S950 and an Atari ST running Cubase were the bedroom studio, and breakbeat hardcore was six seconds of the Winstons' "Amen, Brother" chopped into its hits. The S1000's timestretch — slowing a sample without lowering it — produced the metallic smear on Goldie's "Terminator" in 1992, and jungle had its signature. Shut Up and Dance's "Raving I'm Raving" is the era's cautionary tale: a hit built on a sample nobody cleared.

The Atari and the MPC

The Atari ST had MIDI sockets built in and cost less than a sampler; from 1988 to the mid-90s it ran Cubase or Notator in almost every studio in this book. The Akai MPC60 (1988), Roger Linn's pads-and-swing box, was hip-hop's instrument and the source of the shuffle UK garage later borrowed. Korg's M1 (1988) gave house its piano and its organ bass for a decade, and the E-mu SP-1200 gave hardcore its grit.

What it changed

The sampler made the record the raw material of the next record: breaks, vocals, film lines, other people's riffs. It made production a matter of selection and editing rather than playing, and it made the law a part of the music. The Anatomy strand's sampling chapter lets you chop a break yourself.

Technology · 03 · 1995–2001

Virtual analogue and the system

By 1995 the originals were expensive again and digital chips could imitate them: Clavia's Nord Lead, Access's Virus and Roland's JP-8000 with its supersaw are the sound of the trance boom and of minimal's first years. Meanwhile Tony Andrews's Funktion-One made the club itself an instrument.

The red keyboard and the supersaw

The Nord Lead (1995) was the first virtual-analogue synth and the lead on a thousand late-90s records; the Access Virus (1997) was Armin's, Tiësto's and later Berlin's workhorse; the JP-8000 (1996) put seven detuned sawtooths in one oscillator and the supersaw became trance's lead, hard techno's in the 2020s. Novation's Bass Station (1993) stood in for the 303 and 101 in drum & bass and acid techno.

Audio in the computer

Cubase VST (1996) put audio tracks and plug-in effects inside the computer; Logic and Pro Tools followed; by 2000 a record could be made with no hardware at all. ReBirth (1997) was a 303, an 808 and a 909 on a screen; FruityLoops (1997), a Belgian step sequencer for Windows, became the first DAW of a generation. The hardware era ends here, though nobody noticed for a few years.

The system

Funktion-One (1992), out of Turbosound, horn-loaded and efficient, became the standard for the rooms that cared: Space's terrace, fabric's bodysonic floor, Berghain's main hall. The club's sound system, which the Paradise Garage had made the point, became a brand.

Technology · 04 · 2001–2012

The laptop

Three things arrived in 2001: Ableton Live, written in Berlin by Monolake's Robert Henke; the Pioneer CDJ-1000, the first CD deck that felt like a turntable; and Final Scratch, timecode vinyl that let a laptop play through a Technics. The DJ booth and the studio changed in the same year.

Ableton and minimal

Live was a sequencer that could be performed: loops launched in time, warped to tempo, effects played by hand. Richie Hawtin's DE9: Closer to the Edit (2001) — three CDs of other people's records cut into fragments — was its demonstration, and minimal's decade sounded the way it did because the tool made micro-editing and endless loops easy. Villalobos's and Hawtin's marathons, the Allen & Heath Xone:92's four bands and two filters, and the Model 1 mixer that followed in 2016 are the same idea in hardware.

The CDJ and the end of the record box

The CDJ-1000 (2001) was the booth standard by 2005; the CDJ-2000 (2009) played from USB sticks and made the record box a thumb drive. Serato Scratch Live (2004) was hip-hop's timecode, Traktor techno's. Beatport (2004) replaced the record shop, SoundCloud (2007) the demo tape, Discogs the memory. Vinyl survived as an object and a position.

FL Studio and the next generation

FruityLoops grew into FL Studio and, with Reason, became the first DAW of the producers born after 1985: hardstyle, jump-up, dubstep, and half of the 2010s' charts. The bedroom no longer needed the bedroom.

Technology · 05 · 2012–2026

Hardware comes back

The laptop won and then the knobs returned: Elektron's boxes, the Eurorack modular revival, Roland's own remakes of the 808 and 909, and Behringer's exact clones for less than a night out. The machines of 1982 became the machines of 2020, and the live set replaced the laptop set as the thing to see.

Elektron and modular

The Machinedrum (2001) and Octatrack (2011) from Sweden made hardware playable again; Surgeon, Blawan (as Karenn) and the industrial generation built live sets on them. Doepfer's Eurorack format (1996) became, after 2010, a global modular revival: patch cables on stage, the hypnotic lineage's instrument, a whole economy of small manufacturers.

Clones

Roland's TR-8 (2014) and the Boutique TB-03 and TR-09 (2016) were digital remakes of its own failures; Behringer's RD-8, TD-3 and Model D (2017–) were exact analogue copies at a tenth of the price. Forty years after the bargain bin, the bargain bin is new in the box. Hard techno's 2020s sound — hoovers, supersaws, 909 snare rolls — is made on them.

The stream and the browser

Boiler Room (2010) turned the set into a record: Sherelle's 2019 set, Kraviz's, Dekmantel's stages, watched by millions. And the synthesiser moved into the web page: this book's engine is a Web Audio synthesiser, every kick, 303 and hoover generated in your browser from the same ideas as the machines above. The Anatomy strand is the manual; the Mixer is the set.

Anatomy · 00 · The map

Frequency real estate

A techno mix is a negotiation over about ten octaves. Each element is given a slot and the discipline of the genre is that almost nothing is allowed to overlap. Everything on this page comes back to this picture.

Horizontal axis is frequency on a logarithmic scale (each gridline is an octave). Bars show where each element carries most of its energy. Click a bar to jump to that section.

The grid

Techno is 4/4, almost always between 125 and 145 BPM, built on a 16-step grid per bar. The kick lands on every quarter note. Everything else is defined by its relationship to the kick: on it, between it, or against it.

The hierarchy

Kick → rumble/sub → bass → percussion → synths → samples. Loudness, priority and mixing order all follow this list. The kick is tuned first; everything else is tuned to it.

The chain

Each layer here follows the same arc: generate (oscillator, noise, or sample) → shape (envelope, filter) → colour (saturation, FX) → place (level, EQ, sidechain, pan).

Anatomy · 01 · Kick

The kick is the track

In techno the kick is the loudest element, the tuning reference and the thing everything else is carved around. Get this right and the track half-mixes itself.

How it's generated

Nearly every techno kick, analogue or digital, is the same four ingredients:

  • A sine (or near-sine) oscillator for the body. Its resting pitch — the fundamental — is typically 40–60 Hz.
  • A pitch envelope: the oscillator starts high (150–300 Hz) and sweeps down to the fundamental in 20–80 ms. The speed of this sweep is the "punch".
  • An amplitude envelope: instant attack, exponential decay. Short decays (150–300 ms) are tight and dry; long decays (400–800 ms) become a boom.
  • A click transient — a few milliseconds of filtered noise — so the hit reads on small speakers and cuts through the hats.

Then saturation. Driving the sine into soft clipping adds odd harmonics (3rd, 5th…) so the kick has presence at 150–400 Hz, not just sub. This is why distorted kicks sound "bigger" on a phone even though the sub is identical.

Three families

909

The Roland TR-909: a triangle-ish oscillator with a fast pitch sweep, noise click, and a hard, punchy decay. The default sound of Detroit, Birmingham and most of the '90s. Try: short pitch decay, medium amp decay, drive ~2.

808 / Sine

Long decay, slow sweep, almost no click: a pure sub boom. Common in slower, deeper and dub-leaning techno where the kick is also the bass. Try: pitch decay 0.08, amp decay 0.7, drive 1.

Distorted / layered

The industrial and Berlin sound: a tuned sine body layered with a heavily saturated or sampled top layer, often from a different source. Try: drive 6+, click high, then add the rumble in section 04.

Tuning

Set the kick's fundamental to the root of the track, or to the fifth, so the sub bass and the kick reinforce rather than beat against each other. Useful anchors: E1 = 41.2 Hz, F1 = 43.7 Hz, G1 = 49 Hz, A1 = 55 Hz. Most club systems roll off below ~35 Hz, so a fundamental under 40 Hz is wasted energy and a lot of headroom.

Anatomy · 02 · Drums

Hats, claps and the 909 kit

The kick says where the beat is. The hi-hats say how fast it is and the clap says which beats matter. Together they're the skeleton every other layer hangs from.

Hi-hats: metal from squares

The classic 808/909 hat recipe, still the one most techno uses, generates "metal" without any samples: six square-wave oscillators tuned to inharmonic ratios are summed, pushed through a band-pass filter around 8–10 kHz to pick out a cluster of partials, then a high-pass to remove anything low. The result is a dense, noisy cluster that reads as a cymbal.

A closed hat and an open hat are the same sound with a different amplitude decay: ~40 ms versus 200–400 ms. In the groove, closed hats run on 8ths or 16ths; the open hat sits on the offbeat 8th — the "and" of each beat — producing the boom-tss engine of 90% of techno.

The six square-wave partial clusters (orange) and the band-pass window that selects which of them you hear. Move the tone slider to shift the window.

Clap amplitude envelope: three or four bursts about 10 ms apart (the "many hands") followed by a longer tail. The eye reads the bursts as the attack and the tail as the room.

Claps and snares

A clap is filtered noise with a stuttered attack: several 10 ms bursts, then a single longer decay. A snare adds a pitched body (a sine around 180–220 Hz) under the noise. In techno the clap or snare lands on beats 2 and 4 — or nowhere. Leaving it out entirely is a defining choice of the Berlin and hypnotic styles, where the kick and rumble carry the weight and a clap would make it feel like house.

Where they go

  • Closed hats: 16ths, often with velocity accents on the offbeats; or 8ths for a sparser, heavier feel.
  • Open hat: the "and" of every beat (steps 3, 7, 11, 15). Shorten the decay as the track gets harder.
  • Clap: beats 2 and 4. Doubling it with a snare gives the Detroit snap; a reverb tail on only the clap gives depth without washing the kick.
  • Ride: a long open-hat-type sound on every 8th, the engine of the harder, faster styles.

Anatomy · 03 · Percussion

Groove lives between the beats

Kick and hats define the grid. Percussion defines the feel — the question-and-answer against the kick that makes a loop roll for eight minutes without boring anyone.

Three sound families

  • Toms / pitched hits: a sine with a quick pitch drop (like a tiny kick) at 100–300 Hz. The 909 toms, congas, bongos.
  • Metallic hits: FM or ring modulation — one sine modulating the pitch of another at an irrational ratio (here 1 : 1.41) gives the clanky, inharmonic "pipe" sounds of industrial techno.
  • Shakers / noise: band-passed noise with a soft attack. Shakers on 16ths add movement without adding weight.

Where to put them

Off the grid. The strongest percussion placements are the 16th before the beat (the "a") and the 16th after it (the "e"), which tug against the kick. Then:

  • Polymeter: a pattern of 3 (or 6) steps looping against the 16-step bar. It only realigns every three bars, so the loop sounds like it is evolving when nothing is changing. This is the single most-used trick in hypnotic and rolling techno.
  • Swing: delaying every second 16th by a fraction of a step. Techno uses much less than house — 52–56% rather than 60%+ — but a little on the percussion only, with the kick straight, is the classic feel.
  • Ghost notes: very quiet hits filling the 16ths you don't hear consciously but feel as "roll".

Anatomy · 04 · Rumble

The Berlin rumble

Not a sound you add — a sound you grow out of the kick. The rumble is the kick's own reverb tail, distorted, low-passed and ducked by the kick itself, so it swells into the gap between hits. It is what makes a club system feel like a room rather than a row of speakers.

The chain

Each stage does a specific job:

  • Distortion first turns the clean sine tail into something with harmonics, so the reverb has texture to smear.
  • The reverb creates the sustained wash. A plate or a small dark room, 0.5–2 s, works; a huge hall becomes soup. A dotted-8th delay instead of reverb gives a rhythmic, pumping rumble.
  • The low-pass throws away everything but the sub-to-low-mid tail, so the rumble sits under the kick and doesn't fight the hats or synths.
  • Sidechain keyed from the kick punches a hole for every hit. Without it, the rumble masks the kick's transient and the whole low end turns into mud. With it, the rumble breathes in time with the track.

The rumble replaces the sub bass in a lot of Berlin-style techno — the tuned tail of the kick is the bassline — which is why those tracks can feel enormous with only three elements.

Anatomy · 05 · Bass

Three jobs, three basses

"Bass" in techno is three different instruments. The sub holds the root. The offbeat bass bounces against the kick. The acid line is a melodic, squelching lead that happens to live in the bass register.

1 · Sub

A pure sine on the root note, usually on the 1 (or held for the bar) and sidechained to the kick. It must be tuned to the kick's fundamental — the same note or a fifth — or the two beat against each other. Mono, no reverb, no stereo widening, ever. Adding a little saturation gives it harmonics so it is audible on small speakers.

2 · Offbeat bass

A short, filtered sawtooth on the "and" of every beat — the exact gap the kick leaves. Because it never overlaps the kick, it can be loud without sidechain. This is the engine of '90s Euro techno, Umek, and most peak-time Drumcode tracks: kick-bass-kick-bass, the oompf-tss with the bass on the tss.

3 · Acid (the 303)

Roland's TB-303 is one oscillator (saw or square), a very resonant low-pass filter, and an envelope that sweeps the filter cutoff on every note. What makes it acid is the sequencer: accents (louder, more filter sweep) and slides (the pitch glides into the next note instead of retriggering). Resonance up, cutoff moving slowly with your hand, and the same 16 notes become a six-minute track.

The 303 filter's response at the resting cutoff (dim) and at the peak of the envelope sweep (bright). Resonance sets the height of the peak; env amount sets how far the peak travels on each note.

Anatomy · 06 · Synth

Subtractive synthesis, four ways

Almost every techno synth sound comes out of the same block diagram: a harmonically rich oscillator, a filter that removes some of it, envelopes and LFOs that move the filter over time. What changes between a stab, a pad, an arp and a dub chord is only how fast things move.

The subtractive signal flow. Modulators (bottom row) don't make sound; they move the parameters of the blocks above them.

Stab

A minor chord played by detuned sawtooths with a fast filter envelope and a short decay: percussive, dark, Detroit. Detune (two oscillators a few cents apart) is what makes it wide; the filter envelope closing quickly is what makes it a stab rather than a chord. Place it on syncopated 16ths, or on the offbeat as a "bass stab" in the mid register.

Pad

The same oscillators with a slow attack (0.5–2 s), the filter opened gently by an LFO, and reverb. Pads in techno are texture, not harmony — one chord, often a bare fifth, sitting for 32 bars under everything, then removed for the breakdown.

Arpeggio / sequence

Short notes from a single oscillator, cycled through a scale by a sequencer, with the filter sweeping over bars rather than notes. The trance-adjacent end of techno (and much of Drumcode) is built on these.

Dub chord

The Basic Channel / Chain Reaction sound: a minor-7th or 9th chord stab fed into a long, filtered delay with heavy feedback, so each hit smears into an echoing cloud that gets darker as it decays. The chord barely matters; the delay is the instrument.

Anatomy · 07 · Sampling

Everything is raw material

Techno has always been a sampling music — drum machines sample reality, and producers resample themselves. The texture that separates a finished track from a preset demo almost always comes from this section.

Sources

  • One-shots: drum hits from machines and records, layered under or over synthesised drums.
  • Vocals: a single word or breath, pitched down, filtered, repeated. Often unrecognisable.
  • Found sound: field recordings, machinery, film dialogue. The industrial lineage lives here.
  • Yourself: print your own loop to audio, then treat it as a sample. This is resampling — the most important technique in this section.

Techniques

  • Chop: slice a loop to the grid, then play the slices in a different order. Rhythm from material that had none.
  • Repitch (varispeed): change playback speed, so pitch and length change together — the sound of old samplers and of every pitched-down vocal. Try the rate slider.
  • Time-stretch: change length without changing pitch (your DAW's warp). The artefacts — grainy, metallic smearing — are frequently the point.
  • Reverse: a reversed hit swells into the beat; a reversed reverb tail is the classic pre-drop riser.
  • Resample: render → reload → process again. Each pass adds a generation of character and commits you to decisions, which is how loops stop sounding like presets.

Anatomy · 08 · Mix

Carving, ducking, placing

A techno mix is mostly subtraction. Every element has already been given its slot on the map; mixing is enforcing it — then using sidechain to let the kick through, and saturation to glue what's left.

Order of operations

  1. Gain stage from the kick. Kick peaking around −6 dBFS, everything else brought in underneath it. If you need to turn the kick up later, turn everything else down instead.
  2. High-pass everything that isn't kick, sub or rumble at 100–200 Hz. Hats at 300+. This single move fixes most muddy techno mixes.
  3. Decide who owns the sub. Either the kick (then the bass is high-passed above the kick's fundamental, or sidechained hard), or the sub bass (then the kick is shorter and the sub holds the note). Not both.
  4. Sidechain the rumble, sub, pads and reverb returns from the kick. Short attack, release set so the gain recovers just before the next kick — at 132 BPM that's under 450 ms, usually 150–300.
  5. Mono below ~150 Hz. Width goes on hats, pads, delays and the top of the stabs. A stereo sub cancels on a club system.
  6. Saturate the busses (drum bus, synth bus) lightly rather than the master heavily. Tape or tube-style saturation adds the harmonics that make it loud without a limiter.
  7. Master last, and gently. A clean limiter to −1 dBTP; club techno typically sits around −8 to −6 LUFS short-term, but the DJ's mixer and the system will do the rest. Don't chase loudness at the expense of transients — the kick's click is the first thing a limiter eats.

Sidechain, heard

Kick, rumble, sub and a stab — the same loop twice. Without the sidechain the low end smears and the kick's transient disappears; with it, every hit punches a hole and the rumble refills it.

Arrangement: the DJ tool

Club techno is arranged to be mixed, not listened to from the start. A 32-bar intro of drums only lets the DJ beat-match; layers are added or removed every 16 or 32 bars; one breakdown around two-thirds of the way through gives the room its moment; a 32-bar outro strips back to drums for the mix-out. Seven minutes, almost nothing happening, and every change feels like an event because the grid never moves.

What to listen for

How the kick changes character between dialects at the same volume; how the rumble (purple) fills Berghain and vanishes in Detroit; how 16 bars feels long at 122 and short at 152; and how a bad bass swap sounds — then do a good one.

The DJ's checklist, for any set

  • Phrasing: techno moves in 16- and 32-bar blocks. Start the incoming track on a phrase boundary so its changes line up with the outgoing one.
  • Bass swap: only one kick and one sub at a time. Kill the incoming low, bring in hats and mids first, swap lows on the phrase, then clear the outgoing track's highs.
  • Key: mix within a key, a fifth apart, or deliberately against it. Each preset shows its root; the Anatomy chapter on bass explains why the kick is tuned to it.
  • Tempo arc: a club set rises slowly — 2–3 BPM per transition — and never drops more than once. The default programme rises 30 BPM across eleven segments.
  • Energy, not volume: add elements and open filters rather than pushing faders; the limiter at the end of the chain punishes loudness with lost transients.
